# Shared neural machinery for sequence learning and hierarchical or conceptual structure: the empirical evidence

Verification note for the report writer. The shared web-search budget ran out after the first two rounds of searching, so later verification used direct retrieval of known documents and DOI resolution rather than new searches. Each finding carries one of two tags:

- **[R]** = read in this session (full text, publisher abstract, or a primary review that reports the finding). Details can be quoted with normal care.
- **[M]** = the citation (authors, year, title, venue) was confirmed by resolving its DOI, but the description of the finding comes from the researcher's prior knowledge of the paper and was not re-read in this session. Treat numbers and fine detail in [M] items as needing a check before they are quoted.

"Shared" is used in four different senses in this literature, and the notes label each study accordingly: **same region** (overlapping activation or lesion site), **same circuit motif** (the same kind of loop or cell type, possibly in different places), **same computation** (the same algorithm, possibly in separate tissue), and **analogy** (a formal resemblance with no neural claim).

## 1. Lashley (1951): do sequences require hierarchical plans, and how has the argument held up?

### Takeaway
Lashley's negative claim (serial behaviour is not an associative chain) is the settled part. His positive claim has split into three separable ideas with very different support: parallel pre-activation of ordered elements (well supported by electrophysiology), chunked or tree-like motor programs (supported, though recurrent networks reproduce the behaviour without explicit trees), and action recursion equivalent to linguistic recursion (not established).

### Cited Findings
- [R] Fitch and Martins summarise Lashley's case: stimulus-response chaining cannot explain goals and subgoals that persist while subtasks run or are interrupted; omission errors do not halt a sequence as chaining predicts; the same elements occur in different orders ("tire" / "right"); and language and music need the same kind of ordering, which Lashley proposed is inherited from motor planning. — [Fitch & Martins 2014, Ann NY Acad Sci 1316:87-104](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4285949)
- [R] Fitch and Martins insist on a strict definition: a hierarchy is a rooted tree, not merely one brain region sitting above another. They separate hierarchical sets (ordered levels, unordered members, such as the parts of a face) from hierarchical sequences (order matters within levels), and they treat recursion (self-embedding) as a distinct property that the field uses loosely. Strictly sequential structure is finite-state; hierarchical sequences need extra memory such as a stack ("supraregular"). — [Fitch & Martins 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4285949)
- [R] Their neural conclusion is hedged: lateral premotor cortex including Broca's area plays "important roles" in hierarchical processing in language, music and "at least some action sequences"; the data are "consistent with overlap" but the link "remains controversial". They add that many action hierarchies have fixed, shallow depth that chaining could explain, that it is debated whether Broca's activity is specific to hierarchy or also appears for non-hierarchical sequences, and that group-averaged fMRI can create false overlap. Their hypothesis is "same region, same computation": the inferior frontal gyrus supplies a scannable buffer needed for supraregular processing in any domain. — [Fitch & Martins 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4285949)
- [M] Rosenbaum, Kenny and Derr (1983) had people produce rapid finger-tapping sequences and found that inter-response times and errors tracked the number of nodes to be traversed in a tree-shaped motor program, the classic behavioural evidence for hierarchical control of movement sequences. — [Rosenbaum, Kenny & Derr 1983, JEP:HPP 9:86-102](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.9.1.86)
- [M] Rosenbaum and colleagues' retrospective concludes that Lashley's core claims survived: plans for sequences are hierarchically organised, and new plans are built by modifying recently used ones. — [Rosenbaum, Cohen, Jax, Weiss & van der Wel 2007, Hum Mov Sci 26:525-554](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.humov.2007.04.001)
- [R] Restle's 1970s behavioural work showed that simple action sequences are stored in a compressed form that requires an internal tree, not an associative chain. — reported in [Dehaene et al. 2015, Neuron 88:2-19](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [M] Lashley's idea that the elements of a sequence are prepared together before execution has direct support. In monkeys drawing shapes, prefrontal ensembles represented all upcoming segments in parallel, with strength graded by serial position (Averbeck et al. 2002). In human MEG, a graded parallel pre-activation of upcoming finger presses ("competitive queuing") predicted skill (Kornysheva et al. 2019). — [Averbeck et al. 2002, PNAS 99:13172-13177](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.162485599); [Kornysheva et al. 2019, Neuron 101:1166-1180](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.01.018)
- [M] Human fMRI pattern analysis of trained finger sequences found single presses represented in primary motor cortex, with chunk-level and whole-sequence-level representations in premotor and parietal cortex. The levels exist but overlap spatially rather than occupying separate areas. — [Yokoi & Diedrichsen 2019, Neuron 103:1178-1190](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.06.017)
- [R] Human fMRI suggests chunking in action planning may be hierarchical, with "chunks of chunks" engaging progressively more anterior prefrontal areas (citing Koechlin & Jubault 2006). — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [M] In mice performing a learned left-left-right-right lever sequence, optogenetic manipulation of striatal direct and indirect pathways altered behaviour at the level of subsequences and of single elements separately, which the authors read as a hierarchical organisation of the learned sequence. — [Geddes, Li & Jin 2018, Cell 174:32-43](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.06.012)
- [M] The main theoretical challenge: Botvinick and Plaut trained a recurrent network on routine tasks such as coffee making and showed that hierarchically structured behaviour, and realistic slips, can emerge from distributed recurrent dynamics with no explicit schema hierarchy. Cooper and Shallice replied that explicit hierarchical schemas and goals are still needed. The dispute is unresolved. — [Botvinick & Plaut 2004, Psychol Rev 111:395-429](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.111.2.395); [Cooper & Shallice 2006, Psychol Rev 113:887-916](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.113.4.887)
- [R] The only direct fMRI test of generating a new hierarchical level in a motor sequence (recursive embedding versus iteration versus repetition, 20 participants, identical final movements) found a bilateral motor-imagery and planning network and "no evidence" for lateral prefrontal or inferior frontal involvement. The authors (one of whom co-wrote "Lashley revisited") conclude this "does not support the notion of multi-domain hierarchical generativity in IFG" and suggest the region may instead support general encoding of structured sequences. — [Martins, Bianco, Sammler & Villringer 2019, Hum Brain Mapp 40:2623-2638](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.24549)
- [R] A methods review by the same group concludes that a broad consensus exists for a circuit of inferior frontal gyrus, superior temporal sulcus and arcuate fasciculus, but that "considerable uncertainty remains about the precise computational function(s)" of it, and that hierarchy must be tested against explicit non-hierarchical alternatives. — [Uddén, Martins, Zuidema & Fitch 2020, Top Cogn Sci 12:910-924](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12442)
- [R] Frontoparietal activity at the completion of a step depends on its level in a task/subtask hierarchy (whole task > subtask > within subtask), and this was seen through extended frontoparietal cortex rather than in one specialised area. — [Farooqui, Mitchell, Thompson & Duncan 2012, J Neurosci 32:17373-17381](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0598-12.2012)

### Inferences
- "Hierarchical" carries at least four meanings in this literature: a control ordering among brain regions; part-whole chunk structure in a sequence; a supraregular grammar needing stack-like memory; and recursion proper. Lashley's evidence supports the second. Claims that action shares machinery with syntax need the third or fourth, and that is where the evidence thins.
- The part of Lashley that has held up best is that order is represented separately from the items ordered. That is also the piece with the strongest cross-domain physiology (see ordinal codes in section 5).
- The Botvinick-Plaut result matters for any architecture claim: observing hierarchically structured sequential behaviour does not show that a system contains explicit hierarchical representations.

### Gaps
- Lashley's chapter (in Jeffress, ed., *Cerebral Mechanisms in Behavior*, Wiley, 1951) was not retrieved; its arguments are reported through Fitch & Martins and Dehaene et al.
- The Rosenbaum papers were not re-read, so effect sizes and exact designs are not given.
- No human or animal electrophysiology was found that shows a tree-structured (as opposed to chunked or rank-ordered) motor plan directly.

## 2. Broca's area and inferior frontal cortex: a supramodal hierarchy processor, or adjacent specialised systems?

### Takeaway
Group-level imaging puts language syntax, artificial grammars, music, action chunks and tool use in or near the left inferior frontal gyrus, which motivated the "supramodal hierarchical processor" view. When language regions are localised in individual brains, they do not respond to music, arithmetic, logic, code or action, and the neighbouring non-linguistic responses belong to other networks. The evidence therefore favours "similar computation in adjacent, parallel circuits" over "same tissue", and even the similar-computation claim is disputed.

### Cited Findings

**For shared machinery**
- [R] Greenfield proposed that in the first two years of life a common substrate, roughly Broca's area, underlies hierarchical organisation both in early speech and in manual object combination including tool use; from about age two the cortex differentiates into relatively modular capacities for grammar and for complex object combination. She also argued chimpanzees show the same ceiling on hierarchical complexity in tool use and symbol combination. This is a same-region claim for infancy and a separate-systems claim for adults. — [Greenfield 1991, Behav Brain Sci 14:531-551](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00071235)
- [R] Koechlin and Jubault (fMRI; button presses to coloured squares or letters, with simple action chunks and superordinate chunks of categorisation tasks, designed to separate hierarchical planning from temporal organisation) found Broca's area and its right-hemisphere homologue active when people organised plans of action. They conclude that Broca's area "implements an executive function specialized for processing hierarchical structures in multiple domains of human cognition" and speculate that this system "possibly captures key functional components" of its contribution to language. Same-region and same-computation claim; the link to language is their hypothesis, not a finding. — [Koechlin & Jubault 2006, Neuron 50:963-974](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2006.05.017); press summary at [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/06/060616092642.htm)
- [M] The region-by-level mapping in that paper, as recalled: premotor cortex for single motor acts, posterior Broca's area (BA44) for simple action chunks, anterior Broca's area (BA45) for superordinate chunks, with phasic responses at chunk start and end. Fitch and Martins give a compatible but less clear summary (more anterior regions for superordinate chunks, with BA44 and BA45 responding at transitions and terminations). Check against the paper before quoting. — [Koechlin & Jubault 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2006.05.017); [Fitch & Martins 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4285949)
- [R] Fadiga, Craighero and D'Ausilio argue that the inferior frontal gyrus and ventral premotor cortex are active in language, action execution and observation, and music, and "are tuned to detect and represent complex hierarchical dependencies, regardless of modality and use", a capacity they speculate evolved from premotor (mirror-system) functions. — [Fadiga, Craighero & D'Ausilio 2009, Ann NY Acad Sci 1169:448-458](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-6632.2009.04582.x)
- [R] Patients with frontal aphasia were worse at ordering pictures of human actions than physical events, with lesion overlap centred on BA44 (Fazio et al. 2009). Fitch and Martins caution that most of these patients had extensive damage, so strong inference is premature. — [Fazio et al. 2009, Brain 132:1980-1988](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awp118); caveat in [Fitch & Martins 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4285949)
- [M] Friederici and colleagues contrasted a finite-state grammar, (AB)^n, with a phrase-structure grammar, A^nB^n, in syllable sequences. Violations of the finite-state grammar engaged the frontal operculum; the phrase-structure grammar additionally engaged BA44/45, and the two sites had different white-matter connectivity. — [Friederici et al. 2006, PNAS 103:2458-2463](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0509389103)
- [R] Bahlmann et al. gave people identical syllable strings to be parsed by adjacency ([ge bi][di tu][po ko]) or by nesting (ge[bi[di tu]po]ko); nesting raised activity in left BA44, and also in right premotor cortex, bilateral anterior insula and basal ganglia. — reported in [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019); original [Bahlmann, Schubotz & Friederici 2008, NeuroImage 42:525-534](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2008.04.249)
- [M] Makuuchi et al. separated embedding depth from dependency distance in German sentences: embedding tracked the pars opercularis (BA44), distance tracked the inferior frontal sulcus, which they read as separating syntax from working memory. — [Makuuchi, Bahlmann, Anwander & Friederici 2009, PNAS 106:8362-8367](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0810928106)
- [R] Zaccarella and Friederici (fMRI, 22 analysed) compared two-word determiner phrases with pseudowords ("DIESE FLIRK") against two-word lists ("APFEL FLIRK"). Phrases activated a ventral-anterior subregion of left BA44. The authors call this a confined locus for Merge. Weaknesses they or the data expose: the whole-brain contrast was not significant, the interaction was marginal (p = 0.039), and only German was tested. — [Zaccarella & Friederici 2015, Front Psychol 6:1818](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01818/full)
- [M] Friederici, Chomsky, Berwick, Moro and Bolhuis present BA44 linked to posterior superior temporal cortex by the dorsal arcuate pathway as the neural basis of Merge, and treat it as language-specific and human-specific. — [Friederici et al. 2017, Nat Hum Behav 1:713-722](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0184-4)
- [R] Activity in left inferior frontal gyrus and superior temporal sulcus grows with the number of words in a constituent, including for jabberwocky; the left putamen is also involved. — reported in [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019); original [Pallier, Devauchelle & Dehaene 2011, PNAS 108:2522-2527](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1018711108)
- [R] Dehaene et al. state that the human inferior frontal gyrus "exhibits a remarkable sensitivity to detect all sorts of regularities, from simple repetitions and alternations to complex nested structures", including four-tone AAAB patterns and chance runs in random sequences. — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Uddén and Bahlmann review fMRI and TMS evidence that the left inferior frontal gyrus is engaged in artificial grammar learning whatever the elements are (letters, syllables, shapes) and "across all different grammars tested, whether they include non-adjacent dependencies or mere adjacent dependencies". They propose embedding this in the general rostro-caudal abstraction gradient of lateral prefrontal cortex. — [Uddén & Bahlmann 2012, Phil Trans R Soc B 367:2023-2032](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2012.0009)
- [R] Petersson et al. found Broca's region active during learning of sequences that need no hierarchy, and proposed it is a generic on-line sequence processor. — reported in [Frank, Bod & Christiansen 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479729); original [Petersson, Folia & Hagoort 2012, Brain Lang 120:83-95](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bandl.2010.08.003)
- [R] In fMRI of macaques and humans hearing rule-based nonsense-word sequences, ventral frontal and opercular cortex responded to ordering violations in both species, suggesting these regions "originally evolved to support domain-general abilities involved in sequence processing". — [Wilson et al. 2015, Nat Commun 6:8901](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms9901)
- [M] Music: an MEG study localised the response to harmonic-syntax violations to Broca's area and its right homologue (Maess et al. 2001), and Patel's shared syntactic integration resource hypothesis holds that music and language share integration resources in frontal cortex while keeping separate representations. Fitch and Martins note the MEG localisation is imprecise. — [Maess et al. 2001, Nat Neurosci 4:540-545](https://doi.org/10.1038/87502); [Patel 2003, Nat Neurosci 6:674-681](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1082)
- [R] Martins et al. report that left posterior middle temporal gyrus lesions impair generating new levels in visual (fractal) hierarchies and that this ability "shares cognitive and neural resources with the processing of linguistic syntax". The shared site here is temporal, not Broca's area. — [Martins et al. 2019, Brain 142:3217-3229](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awz242)
- [R] Thibault et al. (Science 2021; fMRI with multivariate pattern analysis, plus training experiments; about 244 participants across experiments according to the authors' press piece) found that using pliers and comprehending complex syntax share activation and similar activity patterns in small parts of the basal ganglia; manual action without a tool and verbal working memory did not. Thirty minutes of tool training improved comprehension of hard sentences relative to video and bare-hand controls, and training on complex (not simple) syntax improved tool use. Summaries of the paper report that the two tasks did not overlap in left inferior frontal gyrus. — [Thibault et al. 2021, Science 374:eabe0874](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abe0874); authors' summary at [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/tool-language-brain-19646/)
- [M] Earlier tool-use work claimed overlap in Broca's area itself (Higuchi et al. 2009) and linked stone toolmaking to inferior frontal activity (Stout et al. 2008). — [Higuchi et al. 2009, NeuroReport 20:1376-1381](https://doi.org/10.1097/WNR.0b013e3283315570); [Stout et al. 2008, Phil Trans R Soc B 363:1939-1949](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2008.0001)

**Against shared machinery**
- [R] Moro's objection, as reported by commentators: functional categories, the main building blocks of syntax, are "unattested in action"; the operations Merge and Move are "hardly instantiated in action", so locality and island constraints have no action counterpart; and actions answer to physical conditions whereas syntax is a mental representation. The syntax-action parallel is "at best a metaphor". Boeckx and Fujita summarise his points as different atomic units and absent locality conditions, and argue his case fails; Leung argues both sides lack rigour and that the "granularity mismatch" between linguistic and neural primitives must be solved first. — [Moro 2014, Trends Cogn Sci 18:109-110](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2013.11.006); [Pulvermüller 2014, Trends Cogn Sci 18:219-220](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2014.01.001); commentaries [Leung 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4288047) and [Boeckx & Fujita 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00627)
- [M] Musso et al. taught adults rules of a foreign language that were either real (structure-dependent) or "impossible" (based on linear position in the word string). Broca's area activity rose with learning only for the real rules. This is evidence that the region distinguishes hierarchical from linear-order rules, which cuts against treating linear sequence learning and syntax as one process there. — [Musso et al. 2003, Nat Neurosci 6:774-781](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1077)
- [M] Fedorenko, Duncan and Kanwisher showed in individual participants that language-selective regions and domain-general "multiple demand" regions lie side by side within Broca's area, so group-averaged overlap there can be an artefact of blurring two systems. — [Fedorenko, Duncan & Kanwisher 2012, Curr Biol 22:2059-2062](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2012.09.011)
- [R] The 2024 review states that language areas "are not engaged when participants listen to music — a rich, hierarchically structured stimulus", and show little or no response to arithmetic, logic puzzles, computer code, working memory and cognitive control tasks, action observation, gestures, or theory of mind. Claims that language shares machinery with "the processing of hierarchically structured inputs" (citing Fitch & Martins, Fadiga, and Tettamanti & Weniger) "have not found empirical support". Language areas respond only weakly to meaningless syllable sequences. — [Fedorenko, Ivanova & Regev 2024, Nat Rev Neurosci 25:289-312](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-024-00802-4)
- [R] The same review argues syntax is not localised to one area: every language region is sensitive to both word meaning and combinatorial structure, different labs have named different areas as "the" syntactic hub, and claims of selective syntactic loss in agrammatism have been questioned. — [Fedorenko, Ivanova & Regev 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-024-00802-4); primary study [Blank, Balewski, Mahowald & Fedorenko 2016, NeuroImage 127:307-323](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2015.11.069)
- [R] Music: across four experiments, language regions including the inferior frontal component responded to music at or below baseline, were insensitive to scrambling or structural violations in music, and people with severe aphasia who could not judge sentence grammaticality judged melodic well-formedness well. — [Chen et al. 2023, Cereb Cortex 33:7904-7929](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhad087)
- [R] Action: individually localised language regions (except the angular gyrus one) were not engaged by action observation or imitation; the authors name Koechlin & Jubault as a target and say the earlier evidence was "indirect, based on observing activation for language and action tasks within the same broad anatomical areas". — [Pritchett et al. 2018, J Neurophysiol 120:2555-2570](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00222.2018)
- [R] Code: comprehension of Python and of the graphical language ScratchJr strongly engaged the bilateral multiple demand network, while the language network responded "weakly or not at all" even though code is structurally similar to language. A companion study found code comprehension overlaps with the fronto-parietal logical-inference network. — [Ivanova et al. 2020, eLife 9:e58906](https://elifesciences.org/articles/58906); [Liu, Kim, Wilson & Bedny 2020, eLife 9:e59340](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.59340)
- [M] Mathematics: algebraic structure processing and advanced mathematical reasoning recruit parietal, inferior temporal and dorsal prefrontal regions rather than perisylvian language areas. — [Monti, Parsons & Osherson 2012, Psychol Sci 23:914-922](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612437427); [Amalric & Dehaene 2016, PNAS 113:4909-4917](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1603205113)
- [R] Nested non-verbal sequences: for spatial sequences with nested geometric rules (fMRI, 20 analysed), complexity tracked bilateral dorsal BA44 and nesting tracked right mid-dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and anterior caudate; but individually localised language voxels "were essentially silent or deactivated" while mathematics-responsive voxels were active. The authors propose "multiple, possibly parallel circuits for tree-like structures in mathematics versus sentence processing", a circuit "distinct from but close to" language areas. — [Wang et al. 2019, NeuroImage 186:245-255](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6592816)
- [R] Motor recursion did not engage inferior frontal gyrus (section 1), and recursive melody generation activated superior temporal gyrus, not inferior frontal gyrus; the authors suggest earlier inferior frontal findings "may reflect processing of unexpected" events. — [Martins et al. 2019, Hum Brain Mapp](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.24549); [Martins et al. 2020, Brain Struct Funct 225:1997-2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00429-020-02105-7)
- [R] Matchin and Hickok place hierarchical lexical-syntactic structure in posterior middle temporal gyrus and assign posterior inferior frontal gyrus the job of turning those hierarchies into "morpho-syntactic sequences, primarily tied to production". On this account Broca's area is the sequencer, not the hierarchy builder. — [Matchin & Hickok 2020, Cereb Cortex 30:1481-1498](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhz180)
- [M] Alternative readings of Broca's-area effects in sentence comprehension: general cognitive control and conflict resolution (Novick et al. 2005) or working-memory and articulatory rehearsal demands (Rogalsky & Hickok 2011). — [Novick, Trueswell & Thompson-Schill 2005, Cogn Affect Behav Neurosci 5:263-281](https://doi.org/10.3758/CABN.5.3.263); [Rogalsky & Hickok 2011, J Cogn Neurosci 23:1664-1680](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.2010.21530)
- [M] Fedorenko and Shain's methodological point is carried in their title: similarity of computations across domains does not imply shared implementation. — [Fedorenko & Shain 2021, Curr Dir Psychol Sci 30:526-534](https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214211046955)

**Dispute about the selectivity evidence itself**
- [R] Drijvers, Small and Skipper argue the localiser approach is circular (the network is defined by a sentences-versus-scrambled contrast and then tested for selectivity in the same regions), that averaging exaggerates selectivity, and that language is "widely distributed throughout the brain". Fedorenko et al. published a reply (not read). — [Drijvers, Small & Skipper 2025, Nat Rev Neurosci 26:189](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-024-00903-0); [reply, Nat Rev Neurosci 26:190-191](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-024-00904-z)

### Inferences
- The same-region claim at Broca's area is weak at fine spatial scale: every individual-subject test listed above found dissociation, and the one positive cross-domain transfer result (Thibault) locates the shared resource in the basal ganglia.
- The same-computation claim survives in a weaker form that Dehaene and colleagues state outright: several parallel inferior frontal and dorsal prefrontal circuits, each building nested structure in its own domain. Selectivity data cannot refute this, because they test where tissue responds, not what algorithm it runs.
- The selectivity evidence has a gap relevant here: the non-linguistic tasks tested in language regions (music listening, arithmetic, code, action observation) were chosen as rich structured stimuli, but none isolates sequence learning or hierarchy building as such inside individually localised language regions. The nested-geometry result (Wang et al. 2019) is the closest test and it came out negative for language regions.
- Two facts pull in opposite directions for a "sequence equals hierarchy" hypothesis: the inferior frontal gyrus responds to every kind of sequential regularity tested, simple or nested (Uddén & Bahlmann; Dehaene), yet it distinguishes structure-dependent from linear-position rules (Musso). The first suggests one graded sequence system; the second suggests hierarchy is treated differently from linear order.

### Gaps
- Koechlin & Jubault's full text was not retrieved; region-by-level details come from secondary summaries and prior knowledge.
- Thibault et al.'s exact basal ganglia nuclei and per-experiment sample sizes were not confirmed from the sources read. A 2026 preprint from Peking University reporting that putamen lesions disrupt both sentence processing and tool use in 100 patients appeared in search results, but the file could not be retrieved, so it is unverified.
- Fedorenko et al.'s 2025 reply to Drijvers et al. was not read.
- No study was found that measures natural-language syntax, action hierarchy and non-linguistic sequence learning in the same individually localised voxels with matched difficulty.

## 3. Basal ganglia: chunking, start/stop signals, and the claim that the same sequencing circuit serves syntax and cognition

### Takeaway
Animal physiology establishes that cortico-striatal circuits mark the boundaries of learned action sequences and treat them as units. Extending this to syntax and thought (Graybiel's cognitive pattern generators, Lieberman, Ullman) is a circuit-motif hypothesis supported mainly by associations in patients, imaging co-activation and one training-transfer study, and it faces direct counter-evidence about what basal ganglia lesions do and do not impair.

### Cited Findings

**Sequencing and chunking in animals (established)**
- [R] In mice learning a self-paced eight-press lever sequence, neurons in dorsal striatum and substantia nigra came to fire selectively before the first or last press. The proportion with start/stop activity rose over training from about 16% to 28% in striatum, 21% to 41% in nigral GABA neurons and 27% to 44% in dopamine neurons; neurons tuned to middle presses were rare. The signal was not explained by timing, speed or reward value. Deleting striatal NMDA receptors prevented both the signal and sequence learning without impairing press rate. — [Jin & Costa 2010, Nature 466:457-462](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3477867)
- [M] Follow-up recordings identified direct- and indirect-pathway striatal neurons and found start/stop activity in both, with sustained versus inhibited activity through the sequence differing by pathway, read as "parsing and concatenation" of sequences into single actions. — [Jin, Tecuapetla & Costa 2014, Nat Neurosci 17:423-430](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3632)
- [R] Dehaene et al. summarise the chunking physiology: as a motor sequence becomes familiar, prefrontal and basal ganglia neurons shift from bursting at every action to firing only at the first and last, "thus signaling chunk boundaries"; they add that this confirms chunking is real but does "not yet indicate whether and how neurons are allocated to the encoding of entire chunks". — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019); primary sources [Graybiel 1998, Neurobiol Learn Mem 70:119-136](https://doi.org/10.1006/nlme.1998.3843), [Jog et al. 1999, Science 286:1745-1749](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.286.5445.1745), [Fujii & Graybiel 2003, Science 301:1246-1249](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1086872)
- [M] Graybiel (1998) proposed that the basal ganglia recode cortically derived information into action chunks, performance units that can be triggered as wholes, by analogy with Miller's memory chunks. — [Graybiel 1998](https://doi.org/10.1006/nlme.1998.3843)
- [M] In rat grooming, striatal neurons coded the serial position of movements within the stereotyped "syntactic chain" rather than the movements themselves. — [Aldridge & Berridge 1998, J Neurosci 18:2777-2787](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-07-02777.1998)
- [M] Striatal activity in freely behaving mice encodes sub-second behavioural "syllables" and their transitions, and dorsolateral striatal lesions disrupt the sequencing of syllables more than their form. — [Markowitz et al. 2018, Cell 174:44-58](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.04.019)

**Extension to cognition and language (contested)**
- [M] Graybiel (1997) argued by analogy with spinal and brainstem central pattern generators that basal ganglia loops with frontal cortex act as "cognitive pattern generators" that organise sequences of thought, and used this to interpret schizophrenia and obsessive-compulsive symptoms. This is a circuit-motif analogy, not a demonstration. — [Graybiel 1997, Schizophr Bull 23:459-469](https://doi.org/10.1093/schbul/23.3.459)
- [M] Lieberman argues that the Broca-Wernicke model is wrong and that circuits through the basal ganglia act as a sequencing engine for motor control, syntax and cognition alike, citing Parkinson's disease, hypoxia on Everest (speech timing and sentence comprehension degrade together) and the FOXP2 family. He presents basal ganglia "reiteration" as the neural alternative to an abstract recursion faculty. — [Lieberman 2002, Yearb Phys Anthropol 45:36-62](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajpa.10171); [Lieberman 2007, Curr Anthropol 48:39-66](https://doi.org/10.1086/509092)
- [R] Ullman's declarative/procedural model assigns the lexicon to declarative memory (temporal lobe) and rule-governed grammar to procedural memory (frontal cortex and basal ganglia). — [Ullman 2001, Nat Rev Neurosci 2:717-726](https://doi.org/10.1038/35094573); [Ullman 2004, Cognition 92:231-270](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2003.10.008)
- [M] The founding evidence was a reported double dissociation: Parkinson's patients were worse at regular (rule-based) than irregular past tenses, Alzheimer's patients the reverse. — [Ullman et al. 1997, J Cogn Neurosci 9:266-276](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.1997.9.2.266)
- [M] Early Huntington's disease patients were impaired at applying rules in both language and arithmetic, taken as support for a striatal role in rule application across domains. — [Teichmann et al. 2005, Brain 128:1155-1167](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awh472)
- [M] In the KE family, a FOXP2 mutation produces orofacial sequencing dyspraxia together with grammatical deficits, with structural and functional abnormalities in the caudate nucleus among other sites. Mice carrying the humanised Foxp2 variant show faster transitions from declarative to procedural performance. — [Vargha-Khadem et al. 2005, Nat Rev Neurosci 6:131-138](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn1605); [Schreiweis et al. 2014, PNAS 111:14253-14258](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1414542111)
- [R] In syntax imaging, the left putamen and caudate nucleus "are also frequently involved", and nested artificial grammars and nested spatial rules also engage basal ganglia or anterior caudate. — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019); [Wang et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6592816)
- [R] Thibault et al. (section 2) is the strongest recent evidence: shared activity patterns in the basal ganglia for tool use and complex syntax plus two-way training transfer. — [Thibault et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abe0874)
- [R] A meta-analysis reported that grammar ability correlates with procedural learning, and lexical ability with declarative learning, in child first language and adult second language; the authors conclude language is learned "by general-purpose neurocognitive mechanisms that preexist Homo sapiens". ([M]: recalled as 16 studies and 665 participants.) — [Hamrick, Lum & Ullman 2018, PNAS 115:1487-1492](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1713975115)
- [R] Serial reaction time meta-analyses find a procedural learning deficit in dyslexia (14 studies, 314 versus 317 participants, standardised mean difference 0.449, 95% CI 0.204 to 0.693) and in specific language impairment (186 versus 203 participants). The authors note all studies are correlational. The dyslexia figures were read in full; the language-impairment sample sizes come from a search summary. — [Lum, Ullman & Conti-Ramsden 2013, Res Dev Disabil 34:3460-3476](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3784964); [Lum, Conti-Ramsden, Morgan & Ullman 2014, Cortex 51:1-10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2013.10.011)

**Counter-evidence**
- [R] Inactivating the motor output of the basal ganglia (sensorimotor globus pallidus internus, muscimol) in two monkeys slowed and distorted individual movements equally for overlearned and random sequences, while fluent predictive execution of the learned sequence was preserved. The authors conclude the basal ganglia motor circuit contributes "to motor execution, but not to motor sequencing or the storage of overlearned serial skills". — [Desmurget & Turner 2010, J Neurosci 30:7685-7690](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0163-10.2010)
- [M] In rats, dorsolateral striatum was found to control the detailed kinematics of a learned skill, not only the selection or ordering of abstract action units. — [Dhawale, Wolff, Ko & Ölveczky 2021, Nat Neurosci 24:1256-1269](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-021-00889-3)
- [M] Longworth et al. tested patients with striatal stroke, Parkinson's and Huntington's disease on past-tense tasks and found no selective deficit for rule-governed forms; they attributed errors to impaired suppression of competing alternatives late in processing, not to a loss of rule application. This directly disputes the Ullman and Teichmann reading. — [Longworth, Keenan, Barker, Marslen-Wilson & Tyler 2005, Brain 128:584-596](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awh387)
- [M] Aphasia after subcortical stroke is largely explained by hypoperfusion of overlying cortex and is reversed by reperfusion (Hillis et al. 2002); an earlier review reached the same conclusion that the basal ganglia have no direct role in language (Nadeau & Crosson 1997). — [Hillis et al. 2002, Brain 125:1094-1104](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awf113); [Nadeau & Crosson 1997, Brain Lang 58:355-402](https://doi.org/10.1006/brln.1997.1707)
- [R] West and colleagues argue the procedural deficit hypothesis has "some problems": in unselected samples the serial reaction time task was not a reliable correlate of language-related ability (r = 0.03), and low reliability of the task undermines group comparisons. Conway and colleagues replied that the critics see "problems that may not exist" and that procedural/declarative and implicit/explicit distinctions are being confounded. — [West, Vadillo, Shanks & Hulme 2018, Dev Sci 21:e12552](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12552); [West, Melby-Lervåg & Hulme 2021, Dev Psychol 57:749-770](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001172)
- [M] The classic memory-systems dissociation places habit and probabilistic sequence-like learning in the striatum and declarative memory in the medial temporal lobe: amnesic patients learned a probabilistic classification task normally while Parkinson's patients did not, and the reverse held for declarative memory of the session. — [Knowlton, Mangels & Squire 1996, Science 273:1399-1402](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.273.5280.1399)

### Inferences
- What is established is a boundary-marking and unitising function for learned action sequences in cortico-striatal loops. Whether the striatum stores or orders the sequence is disputed even within motor control (Desmurget & Turner).
- Graybiel's and Lieberman's proposals are the closest existing neuroscience statements of "one circuit motif reused for motor sequences, syntax and thought". They rest on anatomical parallelism of the loops and on co-occurring deficits, not on recordings of the same operation in cognitive and motor loops.
- Chunking in the striatal sense turns a sequence into a single higher-level unit. That is one step of abstraction over time (part to whole), repeated at most a few levels in the animal data. Nothing in these studies shows unbounded recursion or taxonomic (kind over instance) abstraction.
- Ullman's model, read carefully, predicts a split rather than a unity for the hypothesis under study: sequence and rule learning (procedural, striatal) versus conceptual and lexical knowledge (declarative, temporal lobe).

### Gaps
- Graybiel 1997 and 1998, Lieberman 2002, Longworth 2005, Teichmann 2005 and Hillis 2002 were not re-read; publisher metadata carried no abstracts.
- No study was found that records start/stop or chunk-boundary signals in humans during sentence processing, which is what the shared-motif hypothesis most directly predicts.
- The 2026 putamen lesion preprint (section 2) would be the first causal human evidence for a shared tool-syntax substrate; it is unverified.

## 4. Prefrontal hierarchy: is the rostro-caudal abstraction gradient the same thing as temporal or sequential control depth?

### Takeaway
No single answer exists, because the theories define the gradient differently. Koechlin's cascade model and Fuster's perception-action cycle build temporal extent into the definition of a level. Badre's later work treats policy abstraction and temporal abstraction as separable, finds that no single dimension orders the frontal lobe, and moves the apex of the control hierarchy from the frontal pole back to mid-dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.

### Cited Findings
- [R] The original hypothesis: more rostral frontal areas support more abstract control, with goals translated into movements through rostral-to-caudal flow. Koechlin's cascade model has control propagating from rostrolateral prefrontal cortex to mid-dorsolateral prefrontal cortex to premotor cortex. Badre and D'Esposito (2007, fMRI) manipulated four levels of rule abstraction and saw activity step from dorsal premotor to pre-premotor to mid-dorsolateral to rostrolateral cortex. — [Badre & Nee 2018, Trends Cogn Sci 22:170-188](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5841250); primary sources [Koechlin, Ody & Kouneiher 2003, Science 302:1181-1185](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1088545), [Badre & D'Esposito 2007, J Cogn Neurosci 19:2082-2099](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.2007.19.12.2082), [Badre & D'Esposito 2009, Nat Rev Neurosci 10:659-669](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2667)
- [M] In Koechlin's model the levels are defined by the temporal framing of the control signal: sensory control (the current stimulus), contextual control (the current context), episodic control (the ongoing temporal episode) and, at the frontal pole, branching control (pending past episodes). Abstraction and temporal depth are the same axis by construction. — [Koechlin, Ody & Kouneiher 2003](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1088545); [Koechlin & Summerfield 2007, Trends Cogn Sci 11:229-235](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2007.04.005)
- [M] Fuster's perception-action cycle likewise ties level to time: executive memory is organised hierarchically in frontal cortex, with prefrontal cortex at the top integrating perception and action across time, and higher levels holding more abstract and longer plans. — [Fuster 2001, Neuron 30:319-333](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0896-6273%2801%2900285-9); [Fuster 2004, Trends Cogn Sci 8:143-145](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2004.02.004)
- [R] Badre and Nee list four constructs that early studies confounded: policy abstraction (how many contexts a rule generalises over), relational integration (how many dimensions must be combined), temporal abstraction (how long a context must be sustained across intervening events) and domain generality. Attempts to separate them were "largely inconclusive", and no single one ranks the frontal areas across tasks. — [Badre & Nee 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5841250)
- [R] They treat policy and temporal abstraction as separable: policy-abstraction effects mainly distinguish pre-premotor from mid-dorsolateral cortex, while temporal or episodic demands are tied mainly to rostrolateral cortex. — [Badre & Nee 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5841250)
- [R] Apex revision: monkey connectional asymmetry and human effective-connectivity modelling (Nee & D'Esposito 2016) put the strongest hierarchical influence in anterior mid-dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, falling off in rostrolateral cortex. Badre and Nee conclude that "anterior mid-DLPFC, rather than RLPFC, is the apex of the frontal control hierarchy" and recast rostrolateral cortex as specialised for internally maintained schematic knowledge. — [Badre & Nee 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5841250); [Nee & D'Esposito 2016, eLife 5:e12112](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.12112)
- [R] Lesion evidence for asymmetry: patients show deficits at the level of control matching their lesion and at higher levels, but not at lower ones. — [Badre & Nee 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5841250); primary sources [Badre et al. 2009, Nat Neurosci 12:515-522](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2277), [Azuar et al. 2014, NeuroImage 84:1053-1060](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2013.09.031)
- [R] Sequential control specifically: in an uncued four-step task sequence, rostrolateral prefrontal activity ramped across the sequence and peaked at its end, and TMS there (not over mid-dorsolateral cortex) produced errors late in the sequence. Activity was lowest at the first position although reaction time was longest there, which the authors use against a pure difficulty account. — [Desrochers, Chatham & Badre 2015, Neuron 87:1357-1368](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.08.026), as described in [Badre & Nee 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5841250)
- [R] Sceptical view: Crittenden and Duncan found multiple-demand activity tracking task difficulty with no rostro-caudal hierarchy; Reynolds et al. (2012) found no rostro-caudal differences. Farooqui et al. found sensitivity to task/subtask level spread through frontoparietal cortex. — [Crittenden & Duncan 2014, Cereb Cortex 24:532-540](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhs333); [Farooqui et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0598-12.2012); discussed in [Badre & Nee 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5841250)
- [R] Mechanism: Badre and Nee propose nested cortico-striatal-thalamic loops that gate working memory, with rostral areas biasing more caudal loops; they say whether gating is cortico-cortical, cortico-striatal or both "remains controversial". This ties the prefrontal hierarchy to the basal ganglia motif of section 3. — [Badre & Nee 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5841250)
- [R] The left inferior frontal gyrus may sit inside this gradient: Uddén and Bahlmann propose that structured-sequence processing there follows the same rostro-caudal abstraction principle, a prediction they note "has not yet been tested". — [Uddén & Bahlmann 2012](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2012.0009)
- [R] Monkey prefrontal neurons encode the abstract category of an action sequence (AAAA, ABAB, AABB) regardless of which movements fill it. — [Shima, Isoda, Mushiake & Tanji 2007, Nature 445:315-318](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05470), as described in [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Macaque prefrontal population activity during sequence working memory decomposes into separate low-dimensional subspaces, one per ordinal rank, each storing the item at that rank, and the code generalises to new sequences. — [Xie et al. 2022, Science 375:632-639](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abm0204)
- [R] In mice trained on many tasks sharing an abstract structure (a repeating sequence of goals), most medial frontal neurons tiled "progress to goal", and a subset fired at a fixed task lag from a behavioural step, forming "task-structured memory buffers" that encode the whole future sequence, transfer to new tasks on the first trial, and replay in sleep. Here an abstract schema is implemented as a sequence code. — [El-Gaby et al. 2024, Nature 636:671-680](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08145-x)
- [M] A parallel literature orders the whole cortex by temporal integration window: sensory areas integrate over hundreds of milliseconds, higher-order areas over sentences and paragraphs, and event boundaries nest across levels. — [Hasson, Chen & Honey 2015, Trends Cogn Sci 19:304-313](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2015.04.006); [Baldassano et al. 2017, Neuron 95:709-721](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2017.06.041)

### Inferences
- Whether abstraction depth and sequential depth coincide depends on the theory: yes by definition for Koechlin, Fuster and Hasson; empirically no, or at least not along one axis, for Badre and Nee.
- The physiological results (Shima; Xie; El-Gaby) show prefrontal cortex abstracting over sequences: sequence category, rank-indexed slots and task-structure schemas. This is abstraction of temporal structure. None of these studies tests taxonomic or part-whole conceptual hierarchies with no temporal component.
- The "rank subspace" and "memory buffer" codes are slot-based, not tree-based. They fit Dehaene's level 3 (ordinal knowledge) better than level 5 (nested trees).

### Gaps
- Koechlin 2003 and the Fuster papers were not re-read.
- No study was found that crosses policy abstraction with sequence depth in a factorial design and maps both in the same participants; Badre and Nee describe the separation as incompletely resolved.
- Findings published after Badre and Nee (2018) on the frontal hierarchy were not systematically searched because the search budget ran out.

## 5. Dehaene et al. (2015): five levels of sequence knowledge, and which are shared with other animals

### Takeaway
Dehaene and colleagues argue for at least five distinct mechanisms, not one. The first three (transitions and timing, chunking, ordinal knowledge) are shared with monkeys and present in infants; rudiments of the fourth (algebraic patterns) exist in monkeys; the fifth (nested trees) "may be" uniquely human and needs "a specific recursive neural code, as yet unidentified". Their later work adds that nested-sequence processing runs in circuits parallel to, not inside, the language network.

### Cited Findings
- [R] The taxonomy: (1) transitions and timing; (2) chunking; (3) ordinal knowledge; (4) algebraic patterns; (5) nested tree structures. The authors call these "five distinct cerebral mechanisms" and the minimum any model must emulate. — [Dehaene, Meyniel, Wacongne, Wang & Pallier 2015, Neuron 88:2-19](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Level 1 is replicated across many circuits "that operate automatically and in parallel": mismatch and omission responses appear in auditory and visual cortex, inferotemporal cortex, premotor cortex and basal ganglia, and dopamine neurons; time-coding neurons are found in prefrontal cortex, caudate, cerebellum, parietal cortex and hippocampus. Box 1: transition probabilities and timing "can be represented by a great diversity of neural circuits including PFC, basal ganglia, and cerebellum". — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Level 2: chunks are probably formed "in multiple cortical areas", including inferior frontal and superior temporal regions for auditory chunking and frontal/basal ganglia loops for motor chunking. The authors note "there is nothing in our definition of a chunk which is specific to sequences": faces, objects and places are also chunks. Whole-sequence ("global") regularity detection is found in adults, 3-month-old infants and macaques, and disappears under distraction, sleep or unconsciousness. — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Level 3: humans and monkeys both encode ordinal position independent of timing. As many as 44% of monkey prefrontal neurons in one study were tuned to serial position regardless of item. Ordinal knowledge also arises from unordered pair training (a/b, b/c, c/d, d/e yields knowledge of first, second, last) and transfers to a sequence task, which links transitive inference to sequence order. — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Level 4: "rudiments" are available to non-human primates (Shima et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2015), and infants generalise patterns to new items. The authors know of "no accepted neural network mechanism" for this abstraction. — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019); infant rule learning in [Marcus et al. 1999, Science 283:77-80](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.283.5398.77)
- [R] Species difference at level 4: with identical tone sequences (fMRI, three macaques and 19 humans), monkeys responded to number violations in intraparietal, cingulate and supplementary motor areas and to pattern violations in basal ganglia, ventral prefrontal and temporal cortex, at disjoint sites. In humans the two overlapped in inferior frontal and posterior superior temporal cortex, sites also active in sentence processing. The authors "speculated" that human language areas integrate sequence information into one unified expression. — [Wang, Uhrig, Jarraya & Dehaene 2015, Curr Biol 25:1966-1974](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2015.06.035), as described in [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Level 5: nested trees are needed for language and relate to an inferior frontal and posterior superior temporal circuit, but "the formation of nested trees is by no means unique to language": action, music and mathematics show combinatorial syntax too. The authors offer the "tentative hypothesis" that "multiple parallel IFG areas", with distinct cytoarchitecture and connectivity to posterior cortex and basal ganglia, build trees in different domains. Mathematical expressions engage a "distinct dorsal network" including dorsal BA44. — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Animal evidence on level 5: tamarins learned (AB)^n but not A^nB^n (Fitch & Hauser 2004); claims that starlings and macaques learn nested structures (Gentner et al. 2006; Abe & Watanabe 2011) "have been heavily disputed" because simpler strategies explain the behaviour (van Heijningen et al. 2009). The authors add that the evidence "does not however conclusively demonstrate that nested structures are the key difference between human and non-human brains". — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019); [Fitch & Hauser 2004](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1089401); [Gentner et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04675); [van Heijningen et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0908113106)
- [R] Candidate neural code for trees: vector symbolic architectures, in which a filler vector is bound to a role vector by tensor product and the result can be bound again recursively. The authors point out that this is "similar to a previous proposal for serial order in working memory" (Botvinick & Watanabe 2007), where item identity is multiplied by ordinal rank, and that such conjunctive rank-by-item cells have been recorded in prefrontal cortex. It remains "unsupported by direct physiological observation". — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019); [Botvinick & Watanabe 2007, J Neurosci 27:8636-8642](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2110-07.2007)
- [R] Their open questions include whether human abstraction stems "from a domain-specific system for language processing, or from a broader competence for representing abstract rules, possibly duplicated in several parallel circuits". — [Dehaene et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.019)
- [R] Dissent on human uniqueness: in a touchscreen task ordering brackets, US adults, Tsimane' adults and 3-to-5-year-olds spontaneously produced centre-embedded orders. Three macaques did not at first (47/97), but after extra exposure did overall (49/68), with two of three animals centre-embedding; transfer to new stimuli was marginal (135/244, p = .05) and driven by one animal. Monkeys needed about 1,368 trials per training list. Estimated use of a recursive strategy: US adults 0.82, Tsimane' 0.41, children 0.27, monkeys 0.22. The authors conclude the capacity "is not unique to humans" and link species and age differences to working memory. — [Ferrigno, Cheyette, Piantadosi & Cantlon 2020, Sci Adv 6:eaaz1002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7319756/)
- [M] Macaques also learned to produce mirror (supra-regular) spatial sequences after extensive training, where children needed little. — [Jiang et al. 2018, Curr Biol 28:1851-1859](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.04.047)
- [R] Against monkey compression: in a spatial sequence reproduction task, "only humans, regardless of age, spontaneously extracted the spatial relations between consecutive items and used a chunking strategy to compress sequences"; monkeys did not "even after extensive training" and were fit by a conjunctive rank-by-item model. — [Zhang et al. 2022, J Neurosci 42:850-864](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0603-21.2021)
- [R] Nested rules in human prefrontal cortex and mental compression: see Wang et al. 2019 in section 2 (dorsal BA44, mid-dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior caudate; language voxels silent). The 2021 MEG study found that spatial sequences are encoded with numerical and geometrical primitives and that the decoded signal shows the predicted nested groupings. — [Wang et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6592816); [Al Roumi et al. 2021, Neuron 109:2627-2639](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.06.009), as described in [Dehaene et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.06.010)
- [R] For binary sound sequences, minimal description length in the proposed language predicted memory better than transition probability, chunk complexity, entropy or Lempel-Ziv compression. The fMRI follow-up used language-network and mathematics-network regions of interest and found complexity effects in dorsal inferior prefrontal cortex. — [Planton et al. 2021, PLoS Comput Biol 17:e1008598](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008598); [Al Roumi, Planton, Wang & Dehaene 2023, eLife 12:e84376](https://elifesciences.org/articles/84376); summary in [Dehaene et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.06.010)
- [R] "Symbols and mental programs" (2022) proposes that humans have "multiple internal languages of thought" for mathematics, music, shape and so on, each using discrete symbols composed recursively into "mental programs that encode nested repetitions with variations", and that "these languages rely on cortical circuits distinct from classical language areas". The geometric language activated dorsal BA44 bilaterally, "close to Broca's area", at a site "not activated by sentences" and overlapping arithmetic and mathematics regions. The paper cites Lashley (1951) as the origin of the nested-plans idea. — [Dehaene, Al Roumi, Lakretz, Planton & Sablé-Meyer 2022, Trends Cogn Sci 26:751-766](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.06.010)
- [R] The same paper notes a difference in kind between these languages: geometric and binary-sequence languages "emphasize repetition and symmetry, whereas ... natural languages avoid repetition and rely on antisymmetry". It reports that baboons show no geometric regularity effect while all human groups do, and that "the bulk of the evidence" suggests only humans grasp recursive languages, while acknowledging "a few dissenting data in macaques". — [Dehaene et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.06.010); [Sablé-Meyer et al. 2021, PNAS 118:e2023123118](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2023123118)

### Inferences
- Dehaene's framework is the most developed empirical position near the hypothesis under study, and it supports a specific version of it: the same computation (recursive composition of symbols into compressed programs) serves sequence memory and abstract structure in several domains. It rejects the same-region version: the circuits are parallel and adjacent.
- The framework also says that most sequence learning (levels 1 to 3) is not this mechanism. Transition learning, chunking and ordinal coding are widespread, shared with monkeys, and do not need recursion. A unified recursive mechanism would, on this evidence, cover only the top of the sequence-knowledge ladder.
- The one place where the 2015 review identifies a common formal mechanism between serial order and tree structure is role-filler binding: rank-by-item conjunctive coding for order, and the same binding applied recursively for trees. This is a proposed computation with partial physiological support for the order case only.
- Human uniqueness at level 5 is contested by behavioural data from three monkeys. The dispute is about capacity versus propensity: monkeys can be trained toward centre-embedding with thousands of trials; humans do it spontaneously.

### Gaps
- Al Roumi et al. 2021 and 2023 were not read in full (the eLife page failed to load); their findings come from search summaries and from the 2022 review.
- Direct neural evidence for a recursive code (as opposed to slot or rank codes) is absent; the authors say so themselves.
- Whether the "language of geometry" results generalise beyond one laboratory was not checked.

## 6. Hippocampus and entorhinal cortex: do sequence statistics produce abstract relational or categorical representations?

### Takeaway
Yes, to a point. Exposure to sequences alone yields cluster, graph-distance and map-like codes in hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and inferior frontal cortex, and the hippocampal sequence machinery (theta sequences, time cells, replay) operates over non-spatial and conceptual content. What has been shown is relational and community structure. Taxonomic hierarchy (kinds over instances) and part-whole structure emerging from sequence statistics has been shown in models, not in the brain, and mature conceptual knowledge depends on neocortical systems that survive hippocampal damage.

### Cited Findings

**Sequence machinery in the hippocampus**
- [R] Buzsáki and Moser propose that "mechanisms of memory and planning have evolved from mechanisms of navigation": path integration maps onto episodic memory, landmark-based map navigation onto semantic memory, and "the neuronal algorithms underlying navigation in real and mental space are fundamentally the same". Semantic knowledge is proposed to form as repeated similar episodes lose their context, as allocentric maps form from repeated routes. Theta cycles hold about five to nine gamma-nested assemblies ordered past-present-future, can chunk large stretches of environment, and "a single neuronal algorithm serves both to order events and to provide temporal context". Stated caveats: the claim "does not imply ... that all memories should have spatial components"; most evidence is from rodents; primates rarely show theta rhythmicity and bats have grid cells without it. — [Buzsáki & Moser 2013, Nat Neurosci 16:130-138](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4079500)
- [M] Supporting physiology in rodents: hippocampal assemblies fire in internally generated sequences during delays (Pastalkova et al. 2008); "time cells" tile gaps between events (MacDonald et al. 2011; Eichenbaum 2014); place-cell sequences are compressed within theta cycles (Dragoi & Buzsáki 2006; Foster & Wilson 2007) and replayed forward or in reverse during rest (Wilson & McNaughton 1994; Foster & Wilson 2006). Buzsáki and Tingley argue the hippocampus is a content-free sequence generator. — [Pastalkova et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1159775); [MacDonald et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2011.07.012); [Eichenbaum 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3827); [Dragoi & Buzsáki 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2006.02.023); [Foster & Wilson 2007](https://doi.org/10.1002/hipo.20345); [Foster & Wilson 2006](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04587); [Wilson & McNaughton 1994](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.8036517); [Buzsáki & Tingley 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2018.07.006); review [Foster 2017](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-neuro-072116-031538)
- [M] Lesion evidence in rats that the hippocampus is needed for order and for ordered relations: hippocampal lesions impaired memory for the order of an odour sequence while sparing recognition of the odours (Fortin et al. 2002), and disconnecting the hippocampal system spared learning of premise pairs but abolished transitive inference across them (Dusek & Eichenbaum 1997). Eichenbaum's relational memory theory treats sequence memory and relational inference as products of one hippocampal function. — [Fortin, Agster & Eichenbaum 2002, Nat Neurosci 5:458-462](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn834); [Dusek & Eichenbaum 1997, PNAS 94:7109-7114](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.94.13.7109); [Eichenbaum & Cohen 2014, Neuron 83:764-770](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.07.032)

**Sequence statistics yielding relational and category-like structure**
- [R] Schapiro et al. (2013) showed image sequences generated by a random walk on a 15-node graph with three communities; every node had four neighbours, so transition probabilities were uniform and only community structure could guide parsing. Participants marked event boundaries at community transitions (Experiment 1, n = 30, t(29) = 2.27, p < .05; Experiment 2, n = 10). In fMRI (n = 20), items from the same community had more similar activity patterns in left inferior frontal gyrus, insula, anterior temporal lobe and superior temporal gyrus; only left inferior frontal gyrus survived the region-of-interest follow-up. The hippocampus is not among the reported results. A feed-forward network trained only to predict the next item developed hidden representations clustered by community. The authors propose that items group into events by shared temporal context "just as" objects group into categories by shared features. — [Schapiro, Rogers, Cordova, Turk-Browne & Botvinick 2013, Nat Neurosci 16:486-492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3749823)
- [M] A follow-up found the same community structure in hippocampal activity patterns. — [Schapiro, Turk-Browne, Norman & Botvinick 2016, Hippocampus 26:3-8](https://doi.org/10.1002/hipo.22523)
- [R] Garvert et al.: participants saw objects in an order set by a random walk on a hidden graph (12 objects), with no awareness of structure. Next day, entorhinal fMRI adaptation scaled with graph distance between objects presented in random order (n = 23), with weaker hippocampal effects. The best-fitting metrics were communicability and the successor representation (a weighted sum of future states), not Euclidean distance. No map-like effects survived outside the hippocampal formation. — [Garvert, Dolan & Behrens 2017, eLife 6:e17086](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5407855)
- [R] Human single neurons: hippocampal and entorhinal neurons "gradually modify their activity to encode the temporal structure of a complex image presentation sequence", without instruction; the population geometry "closely resembled the structural graph defining the sequence", and learning was linked to time-compressed replay of graph trajectories. — [Tacikowski, Kalender, Ciliberti & Fried 2024, Nature 635:160-167](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07973-1)
- [M] Intracranial recordings during statistical learning of syllable and shape streams found a division of labour: early sensory cortex tracked transition probabilities, while inferior frontal cortex, anterior temporal cortex and hippocampus represented the identity of learned "words" and ordinal position within them. The authors frame this as hierarchical sequence representations across cortex and hippocampus. — [Henin et al. 2021, Sci Adv 7:eabc4530](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abc4530)
- [R] Necessity: patient LSJ, with complete bilateral hippocampal loss and wider medial temporal damage, showed no statistical learning for shapes, syllables, scenes or tones. In a larger group, hippocampal patients learned less than controls but "were not uniformly at chance"; the authors conclude hippocampal pathology "alone does not abolish such learning". — [Schapiro et al. 2014, J Cogn Neurosci 26:1736-1747](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_00578); [Covington, Brown-Schmidt & Duff 2018, J Cogn Neurosci 30:680-697](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_01228)
- [R] A hippocampal network model handled both: the monosynaptic entorhinal-to-CA1 pathway learned regularities across sequences, while the trisynaptic pathway stored individual episodes. This places statistical and episodic learning in separate pathways within one structure. — [Schapiro, Turk-Browne, Botvinick & Norman 2017, Phil Trans R Soc B 372:20160049](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2016.0049)
- [M] The successor representation account makes the link formal: place cells encode expected future state occupancy learned from experienced transitions, and grid-like codes are a low-dimensional basis of that predictive map. — [Stachenfeld, Botvinick & Gershman 2017, Nat Neurosci 20:1643-1653](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4650)
- [M] Elman's simple recurrent network, trained only to predict the next word, developed hidden representations whose cluster tree separated nouns from verbs and, within nouns, animates from inanimates and so on. This is the classic computational demonstration that sequence prediction can yield a category hierarchy; it is a model result, not brain data. — [Elman 1990, Cogn Sci 14:179-211](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1402_1)

**Spatial codes reused for concepts**
- [R] Constantinescu et al. (fMRI, 28 participants): navigating a two-dimensional "bird space" (neck length by leg length) produced a hexagonally symmetric signal in entorhinal and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, in regions similar to those active in spatial navigation, stable across sessions more than a week apart. — [Constantinescu, O'Reilly & Behrens 2016, Science 352:1464-1468](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaf0941)
- [M] Related human findings: hippocampal signals track distances in learned feature and concept spaces (Theves et al. 2019, 2020), in a two-dimensional social hierarchy assembled from separately learned one-dimensional rankings (Park et al. 2020), and in a novel audiovisual semantic space (Viganò & Piazza 2020). — [Theves, Fernández & Doeller 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.02.035); [Theves, Fernández & Doeller 2020](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0494-20.2020); [Park et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2020.06.030); [Viganò & Piazza 2020](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1849-19.2020)
- [R] Hippocampal theta during word recall coded both semantic distance (word2vec-derived) and temporal distance between words (189 patients, 96 with medial temporal electrodes). The semantic effect appeared only for one-dimensional semantic subspaces. — [Solomon, Lega, Sperling & Kahana 2019, PNAS 116:24343-24352](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1906729116)
- [M] Behrens et al. propose that the hippocampal-entorhinal system factorises structural knowledge from sensory particulars so that structure learned in one setting transfers to another; Bellmund et al. propose that place and grid codes provide a geometric format for "cognitive spaces". Both are frameworks built mainly on spatial data. — [Behrens et al. 2018, Neuron 100:490-509](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2018.10.002); [Bellmund, Gärdenfors, Moser & Doeller 2018, Science 362:eaat6766](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat6766)
- [R] The Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine is a model that learns from sequences of observations and actions. Medial entorhinal units carry an abstract structural code, lateral entorhinal units carry sensory content, and hippocampal units bind them. Trained on spatial grids, transitive-inference lines, family trees and a lap-counting task, it reproduces grid, border, place and lap cells and makes first-trial inferences in new environments. The authors present it as formalising Eichenbaum's claim that spatial and relational memory share one mechanism. Limits they state: it is a computational-level model, trained on simple behaviours, with several predictions untested. — [Whittington et al. 2020, Cell 183:1249-1263](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707106)
- [M] Human replay carries abstract structure: in MEG, after people learned a rule for unscrambling sequences, rest-period replay followed the rule-defined order, not the experienced order, and abstract codes for sequence position and sequence identity led the item codes by tens of milliseconds. Human fMRI also shows sequential replay of non-spatial task states. — [Liu, Dolan, Kurth-Nelson & Behrens 2019, Cell 178:640-652](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2019.06.012); [Schuck & Niv 2019, Science 364:eaaw5181](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaw5181)
- [R] In mice, CA1 cells counted laps as transferable event units on a four-lap task, and the TEM model reproduced this. — [Sun, Yang, Martin & Tonegawa 2020, Nat Neurosci 23:651-663](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-020-0614-x), as described in [Whittington et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707106)

**Limits and counter-evidence**
- [M] Conceptual knowledge can be acquired and retained without a working hippocampus. Children with early bilateral hippocampal damage had severe episodic amnesia but reached low-average to average levels of language, literacy and factual knowledge. — [Vargha-Khadem et al. 1997, Science 277:376-380](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.277.5324.376)
- [M] The converse dissociation: semantic dementia, from anterior temporal lobe degeneration, erodes conceptual knowledge from specific to general (subordinate distinctions are lost first) while day-to-day episodic memory and procedural skills are relatively spared. The dominant "hub-and-spoke" account locates concept structure in anterior temporal neocortex, not in the hippocampal sequence system. — [Patterson, Nestor & Rogers 2007, Nat Rev Neurosci 8:976-987](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2277); [Lambon Ralph, Jefferies, Patterson & Rogers 2017, Nat Rev Neurosci 18:42-55](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2016.150)
- [M] Amnesic patients learned an artificial grammar normally while failing to recognise the training strings, so rule-like sequence learning can proceed without the hippocampal declarative system. — [Knowlton, Ramus & Squire 1992, Psychol Sci 3:172-179](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00021.x)
- [M] Complementary learning systems theory holds that fast, sequence-specific hippocampal learning and slow extraction of category structure in neocortex are computationally opposed and need separate systems, linked by replay. — [McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly 1995, Psychol Rev 102:419-457](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.3.419)

### Inferences
- The direct answer to the key question is a qualified yes. Temporal co-occurrence statistics alone produce cluster-level (community) and metric (graph-distance) representations in humans, in inferior frontal, anterior temporal, entorhinal and hippocampal tissue, without awareness or instruction.
- What these experiments demonstrate is "which items belong together" and "how far apart items are". None demonstrates in neural data a multi-level taxonomy or part-whole hierarchy arising from sequence exposure. The family-tree result is a simulation; the Elman result is a simulation.
- "Shared" here is mostly same-region plus same-computation: the same hippocampal-entorhinal cells and rhythms that order places in space also order events in time and code distances between concepts. This is the strongest empirical case in these notes for one mechanism serving sequence and relational structure.
- The dissociation evidence constrains it: the hippocampal system looks like a fast learner and organiser of relational structure, whose products are consolidated into neocortical semantic systems that then work without it. A single shared mechanism for sequence memory and long-term conceptual order is not what the patient data show; a shared learning front end with separate long-term stores fits better.
- Schapiro's 2013 effect was in left inferior frontal gyrus, the region at issue in section 2. That is a point of contact between the hippocampal and the frontal literatures: frontal "structured sequence" tissue also carries cluster structure learned from sequences.

### Gaps
- Behrens et al. 2018, Bellmund et al. 2018, Liu et al. 2019, Stachenfeld et al. 2017 and Henin et al. 2021 were not re-read; Henin's regional details in particular should be checked.
- Grid-like fMRI signals for conceptual spaces are an indirect proxy for grid cells; replication status and criticisms of the hexadirectional method were not reviewed.
- No study was found that tests whether sequence exposure yields hierarchical (nested community or taxonomic) neural representations in humans.

## 7. Statistical and sequence learning and language

### Takeaway
Sequence-learning measures correlate modestly with language processing in several studies, and agrammatic patients are often impaired at artificial grammars, which supports partly shared mechanisms. The correlations are small and the tasks are unreliable and do not correlate well with each other, so "statistical learning" is probably not one ability. The Christiansen school goes further and argues that language use is largely sequential, with hierarchy a by-product of chunking across timescales.

### Cited Findings
- [M] Eight-month-olds segment words from a continuous syllable stream using transitional probabilities after two minutes (Saffran et al. 1996), and seven-month-olds generalise ABA/ABB patterns to new syllables (Marcus et al. 1999), which Marcus read as rule learning beyond statistics. — [Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996, Science 274:1926-1928](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.274.5294.1926); [Marcus et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.283.5398.77); review [Saffran & Kirkham 2018, Annu Rev Psychol 69:181-203](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-122216-011805)
- [R] A 25-year meta-analysis of auditory-linguistic statistical learning found robust learning in infants and adults, but "significant publication bias", reliance on a narrow range of simplified stimuli, and no clear effect of moderators such as exposure amount and transitional probability strength, results that "contradict core theoretical assumptions in the field". — [Isbilen & Christiansen 2022, Cogn Sci 46:e13198](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.13198)
- [R] Misyak, Christiansen and Tomblin (50 adults): on-line learning of non-adjacent dependencies in an artificial grammar correlated with reading speed at the main verb of subject relatives (r = −0.30, p = .04) and object relatives (r = −0.34, p = .02). The design is correlational, and on-line and off-line learning measures did not correlate with each other (r = 0.15). — [Misyak, Christiansen & Tomblin 2010, Front Psychol 1:31](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00031/full)
- [M] Implicit sequence learning in adults predicted how well they used sentence predictability to perceive degraded speech (Conway et al. 2010). In children, serial reaction time learning predicted persistence of syntactic priming (Kidd 2012), and visual statistical learning predicted comprehension of passives and object relatives (Kidd & Arciuli 2016). — [Conway, Bauernschmidt, Huang & Pisoni 2010, Cognition 114:356-371](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2009.10.009); [Kidd 2012, Dev Psychol 48:171-184](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025405); [Kidd & Arciuli 2016, Child Dev 87:184-193](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12461)
- [R] Patients with agrammatic aphasia were impaired at visual artificial grammar learning, and violations in a purely sequential artificial language elicit EEG responses resembling those to natural-language syntactic violations in the same people. — reported in [Frank, Bod & Christiansen 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479729); primary sources [Christiansen, Kelly, Shillcock & Greenfield 2010, Cognition 116:382-393](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2010.05.015), [Christiansen, Conway & Onnis 2012, Lang Cogn Process 27:231-256](https://doi.org/10.1080/01690965.2011.606666)
- [M] Patients with vascular and progressive non-fluent aphasia were impaired on artificial grammars built from both nonsense words and tones, suggesting a domain-general sequence-processing deficit accompanying agrammatism. — [Cope et al. 2017, Neuropsychologia 104:201-213](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2017.08.022)
- [R] Frank, Bod and Christiansen argue that for language use, as opposed to linguistic description, hierarchical structure is "rarely (if ever) needed". Evidence: overlap between sequence-learning and language regions; Broca's area active for linear sequences; agreement errors that depend on linear rather than hierarchical distance; local coherence effects; recurrent networks without hierarchy predicting reading times better than phrase-structure models; n-gram models learning auxiliary fronting. They sketch a model in which constructions are sequential streams that interleave, with interference capping embedding depth. They concede hierarchy may be computed in attentive or metalinguistic conditions and that the model comparisons are "by no means conclusive". — [Frank, Bod & Christiansen 2012, Proc R Soc B 279:4522-4531](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479729)
- [R] Christiansen and Chater's Now-or-Never bottleneck: input is overwritten unless processed immediately, so the system must "chunk and pass", recoding input into chunks at successively more abstract levels (phonemes, syllables, words, phrases). The bottleneck is said to arise from general perceptuo-motor and memory principles, and acquisition is "learning to process", not grammar induction. On this view levels of linguistic abstraction are layers of chunking over time. — [Christiansen & Chater 2016, Behav Brain Sci 39:e62](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X1500031X); summary at [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/286103)
- [M] A chunk-based learner using backward transitional probabilities reproduces aspects of child language production across many languages. — [McCauley & Christiansen 2019, Psychol Rev 126:1-51](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000126)
- [M] Statistical learning is not one ability: individual scores on visual and auditory statistical learning tasks correlate weakly or not at all with each other, the tasks have modest reliability in adults and poor reliability in children, and learning is constrained by modality. — [Siegelman & Frost 2015, J Mem Lang 81:105-120](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2015.02.001); [Frost, Armstrong, Siegelman & Christiansen 2015, Trends Cogn Sci 19:117-125](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2014.12.010); [Siegelman, Bogaerts, Christiansen & Frost 2017](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2016.0059); [Arnon 2020, Behav Res Methods 52:68-81](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-019-01205-5)
- [R] Meta-analytic and critical findings on procedural (sequence) learning and language ability are in section 3: positive group differences in dyslexia and language impairment, a positive grammar-procedural correlation (Hamrick et al.), and a near-zero correlation in unselected samples (West et al., r = 0.03). — [Lum et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3784964); [Hamrick, Lum & Ullman 2018](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1713975115); [West et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001172)
- [M] Neural tracking of hierarchy is itself disputed. Cortical activity tracks phrase and sentence rates in isochronous speech with no acoustic cues (Ding et al. 2016), and intracranial high-gamma activity builds across the words of a constituent and drops when it closes (Nelson et al. 2017). But a model using only word-level properties, with no hierarchical structure, reproduces the Ding spectral peaks (Frank & Yang 2018). — [Ding et al. 2016, Nat Neurosci 19:158-164](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4186); [Nelson et al. 2017, PNAS 114:E3669-E3678](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1701590114); [Frank & Yang 2018, PLoS ONE 13:e0197304](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0197304)
- [R] Cross-species continuity: the ventral frontal regions that respond to ordering violations in rule-based auditory sequences are conserved between macaques and humans (section 2). — [Wilson et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms9901)
- [M] Macaques and marmosets learn auditory artificial grammars, and non-human primates show a range of sequential learning abilities short of hierarchical structure. — [Wilson et al. 2013, J Neurosci 33:18825-18835](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2414-13.2013); [Conway & Christiansen 2001, Trends Cogn Sci 5:539-546](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613%2800%2901800-3)

### Inferences
- The individual-differences evidence supports a modest overlap between sequence learning and syntactic processing (correlations around 0.3 in the positive studies) and is compatible with no overlap in the critical ones. It cannot carry a strong shared-mechanism claim.
- The Christiansen position and the Dehaene position agree that sequence processing is central but disagree on recursion. For Christiansen, apparent hierarchy is iterated chunking under memory limits and true recursion is marginal in use. For Dehaene, recursive composition is the defining human capacity. A designer assuming "one equivalent recursive mechanism" is closer to Dehaene on mechanism and closer to Christiansen on unity.
- Chunk-and-pass gives a concrete, non-recursive sense in which sequence learning produces levels of abstraction: each level is a recoding of chunks from the level below over a longer time window. This matches the temporal-receptive-window hierarchy in section 4. It yields part-whole structure over time; it does not by itself yield kind-over-instance abstraction.

### Gaps
- Kidd 2012, Kidd & Arciuli 2016, Conway et al. 2010 and Siegelman & Frost 2015 were not re-read; sample sizes and effect sizes are omitted for that reason.
- The published commentaries on the Now-or-Never target article were not reviewed.
- No large preregistered study linking reliable sequence-learning measures to syntax was found; the search budget ended before this could be checked.

## 8. Evidence against a shared mechanism: dissociations, domain-specific networks, and hierarchy that is not recursion

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence against shared machinery is neuropsychological: people with severe agrammatic or global aphasia can still do structure-dependent arithmetic, formal logic, music-structure judgements and theory-of-mind reasoning, and the memory-systems literature doubly dissociates sequence or habit learning, episodic memory and conceptual knowledge. These results rule out a single shared substrate; they do not rule out the same computation implemented in separate circuits.

### Cited Findings
- [R] "Agrammatic but numerate": three men with large left-hemisphere lesions and severe agrammatism (at or near chance on reversible sentences) performed well on arithmetic that parallels syntax. On order-sensitive subtraction and division (59 − 13 versus 13 − 59) they scored 34 to 40 out of 40; on bracketed expressions such as 36 ÷ (3 × 2) they scored 43 to 52 out of 64 with very few serial-order errors; all generated recursive answers on number-infinity tasks. The authors offer two readings: a shared domain-general syntactic mechanism that mathematics reaches without going through language, or separate domain-specific syntactic systems. They note residual grammar might have sufficed and that adult independence does not exclude a developmental role for language. — [Varley, Klessinger, Romanowski & Siegal 2005, PNAS 102:3519-3524](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC552916)
- [M] An agrammatic patient passed causal reasoning and theory-of-mind tasks. — [Varley & Siegal 2000, Curr Biol 10:723-726](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0960-9822%2800%2900538-8)
- [R] Fedorenko and Varley review two lines of evidence: people with global aphasia retain arithmetic, reasoning, music and navigation abilities, and in healthy adults language areas respond to sentences but not to arithmetic or working-memory tasks. They conclude many aspects of thought use systems separate from language. — [Fedorenko & Varley 2016, Ann NY Acad Sci 1369:132-153](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.13046)
- [R] A 2026 PNAS study combined fMRI in healthy adults with behavioural testing of patients who have extensive lesions to language areas (two patients, per the press account). Result: "the language brain network is not engaged during logical reasoning, and patients with severe aphasia exhibit intact performance on logic tasks". Inductive reasoning recruited the multiple demand network; deductive reasoning recruited regions distinct from both. The tasks included inferring a hidden rule that transforms number lists. — [Kean et al. 2026, PNAS 123](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2520095123); press account at [MIT BCS](https://bcs.mit.edu/news/separating-logic-and-language)
- [R] Music: individuals with severe aphasia who cannot judge sentence grammaticality judge melodic well-formedness well, and language regions do not respond to musical structure. — [Chen et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhad087)
- [M] The reverse dissociation exists too: acquired and congenital amusia without aphasia, which Peretz and Coltheart take as evidence for music-specific modules. — [Peretz & Coltheart 2003, Nat Neurosci 6:688-691](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1083)
- [R] Conflicting patient evidence on music: agrammatic Broca's aphasics were impaired on harmonic-syntax tasks. — [Patel, Iversen, Wassenaar & Hagoort 2008, Aphasiology 22:776-789](https://doi.org/10.1080/02687030701803804), as reported in [Frank, Bod & Christiansen 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479729)
- [M] Sequence learning in aphasia is mixed. Some individuals with severe aphasia retain artificial grammar learning (Zimmerer et al. 2014), while group studies find deficits (Christiansen et al. 2010; Cope et al. 2017, section 7). — [Zimmerer, Cowell & Varley 2014, Neuropsychologia 53:25-38](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2013.10.014)
- [R] The network-level picture: the language network, the multiple demand network, the theory-of-mind network and the default network are "robustly dissociated" in individual-subject imaging and after brain damage, and "some individuals with even severe aphasia can nevertheless retain the ability to reason, plan and understand the world". — [Fedorenko, Ivanova & Regev 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-024-00802-4); see also [Fedorenko, Piantadosi & Gibson 2024, Nature 630:575-586](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07522-w)
- [M] Memory-systems dissociations separate the candidate substrates of sequence learning and of conceptual order: striatal habit learning versus medial temporal declarative memory (Knowlton, Mangels & Squire 1996); artificial grammar learning intact in amnesia (Knowlton, Ramus & Squire 1992); semantic knowledge acquired despite early hippocampal damage (Vargha-Khadem et al. 1997); and conceptual knowledge lost in semantic dementia with episodic and procedural memory relatively spared (Patterson et al. 2007). — [Knowlton, Mangels & Squire 1996](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.273.5280.1399); [Knowlton, Ramus & Squire 1992](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00021.x); [Vargha-Khadem et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.277.5324.376); [Patterson, Nestor & Rogers 2007](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2277)
- [M] The systems also interact competitively: during probabilistic classification learning, medial temporal and striatal activity were negatively related, with a shift from the former to the latter over training. — [Poldrack et al. 2001, Nature 414:546-550](https://doi.org/10.1038/35107080)
- [R] Hierarchy in action is not recursion in the linguistic sense: Moro's arguments (no functional categories, no Merge or Move, no locality constraints in action) and Fitch and Martins's point that many action hierarchies are of fixed shallow depth are in sections 1 and 2. The direct tests of generating new hierarchical levels in motor and musical sequences did not engage inferior frontal gyrus. — [Moro 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2013.11.006); [Fitch & Martins 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4285949); [Martins et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.24549); [Martins et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00429-020-02105-7)
- [R] Even proponents of a common computation describe separate implementations: "multiple parallel brain circuits hosting languages with partially distinct programing styles and domain-specific primitives", with natural language relying on asymmetric Merge and antisymmetry and the geometric and sequence languages on repetition and symmetry. — [Dehaene et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.06.010)
- [R] The basal ganglia do not straightforwardly store or order learned sequences (Desmurget & Turner 2010), and structure-dependent rules are treated differently from linear-order rules in Broca's area (Musso et al. 2003); both in sections 2 and 3. — [Desmurget & Turner 2010](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0163-10.2010); [Musso et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1077)

### Inferences
- Summary of where each sense of "shared" stands on this evidence:
  - **Same region:** refuted at Broca's area for language versus music, mathematics, code, logic and action at individual-subject resolution. Supported in the hippocampal-entorhinal system for spatial, temporal and conceptual relations. Reported in the basal ganglia for tool use and syntax, awaiting replication.
  - **Same circuit motif:** plausible and largely untested. Parallel cortico-striatal loops and parallel inferior frontal circuits are anatomical facts; that they run the same operation on different content is a hypothesis (Graybiel, Lieberman, Dehaene).
  - **Same computation:** the live position. Candidate common computations are chunking and compression (Graybiel; Christiansen & Chater; Dehaene), predictive learning of successor structure (Stachenfeld; Schapiro; Garvert), role-filler binding (Botvinick & Watanabe; Smolensky via Dehaene) and structural generalisation by factorising structure from content (Behrens; Whittington). None has been shown by recording to operate identically in sequence and conceptual domains.
  - **Analogy only:** Moro's verdict on syntax and action; also a fair description of Graybiel's cognitive pattern generators as argued in 1997.
- Dissociation evidence is decisive against a single substrate and silent on shared computation. A design that uses one algorithm in separately lesionable modules is compatible with all of it.
- Two asymmetries recur and both tell against a strict equivalence of sequence learning and conceptual order. First, most sequence learning (transitions, chunks, ordinal rank) is shared with other animals and needs no recursion, while nested structure is rare or effortful in animals. Second, the brain systems for learning sequences quickly (striatum, hippocampus) are dissociable from the neocortical systems that hold conceptual knowledge in the long term.
- The evidence most favourable to the hypothesis is that prediction over sequences can generate category-like and map-like representations (Schapiro; Garvert; Tacikowski; Elman's and Whittington's models) and that the same hippocampal-entorhinal code orders both events and concepts. The evidence least favourable is the patient dissociations and the failure to find a common inferior frontal site for hierarchy generation across domains.

### Gaps
- Fedorenko & Varley 2016 was summarised from the abstract and search summaries; the UCL full-text copy returned an access error.
- Kean et al. 2026 was read only as abstract and press account; patient numbers come from the press account.
- Zimmerer et al. 2014 and Peretz & Coltheart 2003 were not re-read.
- No patient study was found that tests, in the same individuals, sequence learning, action hierarchy, syntax and conceptual (taxonomic or part-whole) knowledge. The double dissociations above are assembled across different patient groups and tasks.
- Evidence on part-whole (mereological) and kind-instance (taxonomic) conceptual structure specifically, as distinct from "relational" or "abstract" structure in general, is nearly absent from the sequence-learning literature reviewed here. This is the largest gap relative to the hypothesis that motivated the research.
