# Attentional capture by novelty, onsets, salience and surprise vs. inattentional blindness

Research notes, 2026-10-09. Every citation below was checked against Europe PMC / PubMed metadata (PMID or DOI given) or the publisher's own page unless marked UNVERIFIED. "Primary" = empirical study; "Review" = review/theory paper. Effect sizes are quoted from abstracts or full texts that were actually retrieved; where only an abstract was available this is stated.

Two citation corrections to the brief: (1) Horstmann's 2015 review "The surprise–attention link" is in *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences*, not *Frontiers*. (2) "The attentional capture debate: the long-lasting consequences of a misnomer" is a 2021 *Visual Cognition* commentary by Lamy; Theeuwes' 2023 position paper is "The attentional capture debate: when can we avoid salient distractors and when not?" in *Journal of Cognition*.

## KQ1. Abrupt onsets and new objects: what exactly captures bottom-up?

### Takeaway
The appearance of a NEW PERCEPTUAL OBJECT (not a luminance transient per se, and not a static feature singleton) is the one stimulus class that captures attention in search with no matching attentional set; but even onset capture is not fully automatic: it is defeated by a highly focused attentional state, and its potency is restored by rarity, i.e. by novelty of the onset itself.

### Cited Findings
- Yantis & Jonides (1984), Primary. Search displays in which one item had an abrupt onset while others were revealed by removing camouflage segments; the abrupt-onset item gained a processing advantage (flat search function for onset targets), interpreted as attentional capture by the onset; Exp. 2 ruled out sensory explanations. *JEP:HPP* 10(5):601–621, PMID 6238122 — [Europe PMC](https://doi.org/10.1037//0096-1523.10.5.601)
- Jonides & Yantis (1988), Primary. Abrupt-onset singletons capture attention in search when onset is orthogonal to the target-defining attribute; colour and brightness singletons do NOT ("abrupt onset may be unique in capturing visual attention"). *Perception & Psychophysics* 43(4):346–354, PMID 3362663 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03208805) (abstract text not retrieved; finding summary from secondary sources incl. the 2013 Frontiers review of onset capture, [frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00958/full))
- Yantis & Jonides (1990), Primary. With a central cue directing attention (varying cue duration or validity), "onsets do not necessarily capture attention in violation of an observer's intentions"; a "partially automatic" capture mechanism is proposed. *JEP:HPP* 16(1):121–134, PMID 2137514 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037//0096-1523.16.1.121)
- Yantis & Hillstrom (1994), Primary. Exps. 1–2: capture by a new object EQUILUMINANT with the background (no luminance change); Exp. 3: "a highly salient luminance increment alone is not sufficient to capture attention" — capture "is mediated by a mechanism that detects the appearance of new perceptual objects." *JEP:HPP* 20(1):95–107, PMID 8133227 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037//0096-1523.20.1.95)
- Neo & Chua (2006), Primary. With 100%-predictive central arrow cues, a frequently presented onset "failed to capture attention. But when its appearance was relatively infrequent, capture effects were clearly observed" — rarity (novelty) restores onset capture even under focused attention. *Perception & Psychophysics* 68(8):1286–1296, PMID 17378415 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03193728)
- Folk & Remington (2015), Primary. Infrequent onset precues captured attention for COLOUR-defined targets (violating contingent capture), while infrequent colour singletons did not capture for onset-defined targets; in RSVP, infrequent peripheral onsets decremented target report. Conclusion: "infrequent onsets constitute a special stimulus class producing involuntary, cognitively impenetrable attention shifts." *JEP:HPP* 41(4):1153–1165, PMID 26030438 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000084)
- Luck, Gaspelin, Folk, Remington & Theeuwes (2021), Review (five-author consensus). Folk & Remington's reading of the above: "abrupt onset stimuli would not capture attention when they occurred frequently, but would when they were rare. This was attributed to underlying brain mechanisms that respond to novelty, but that habituate rapidly." Whether onsets can be proactively suppressed is listed as an open question ("challenging because most attentional capture tasks use search displays that suddenly onset"). *Visual Cognition* 29(1):1–21, DOI 10.1080/13506285.2020.1848949 — [PMC7872136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7872136)
- Theeuwes (2023), Review. "Elements appearing with an abrupt onset did have a special status in capturing attention irrespectively of the top-down goal." *Journal of Cognition* 6(1):35 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.251)

### Inferences
- The bottom-up primitive is "new object appeared here", a temporal-structural event, not feature contrast. For a serial text reader the analogue is the arrival of a new token/word-whole, which by this literature should carry a default priority bump.
- Onset capture has two modulators that matter for the design: (i) a sufficiently narrow/valid attentional set can block it (Yantis & Jonides 1990); (ii) frequency/rarity of the onset class modulates it (Neo & Chua 2006; Folk & Remington 2015) — a habituating novelty term sits on top of the onset signal.

### Gaps
- Quantitative RT costs (ms) for onset capture were not retrieved from the primary abstracts; the 1984/1988 papers report search-slope differences rather than single-number costs.

## KQ2. Salience-driven capture vs contingent capture: evidence, controversy and the 2021 consensus

### Takeaway
After ~30 years the three camps agree on a two-stage picture: salient singletons (and onsets) AUTOMATICALLY generate a priority ("attend-to-me") signal, but actual capture can be prevented by a suppression/control mechanism when the system is appropriately configured; what remains disputed is whether suppression is proactive and feature-based (signal suppression) or only reactive/spatial, and whether "feature search mode" works by suppression or by reducing display salience.

### Cited Findings
- Theeuwes (1992), Primary. Additional-singleton paradigm (search for a green circle; an irrelevant unique-colour or unique-form item present): "complete top-down selectivity toward a particular feature was not possible, not even after extended and consistent practice"; selectivity depends on relative discriminability of dimensions. *Perception & Psychophysics* 51(6):599–606, PMID 1620571 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03211656)
- Folk, Remington & Johnston (1992), Primary. Spatial-cueing variant: invalid abrupt-onset precues produced costs for onset-defined targets but not colour-defined targets; invalid colour precues produced greater costs for colour targets than onset targets; "attention capture is contingent on attentional control settings induced by task demands." *JEP:HPP* 18(4):1030–1044, PMID 1431742 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.18.4.1030)
- Bacon & Egeth (1994), Primary. Heterogeneous-shape displays that prevent "singleton detection mode" and force "feature search mode" eliminate capture by colour singletons; "top-down selectivity is indeed possible during visual search." *Perception & Psychophysics* 55(5):485–496, PMID 8008550 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03205306)
- Theeuwes (2010), Review. The stimulus-driven position: the most salient item in the attended window automatically drives the first shift; top-down control acts only later or by shrinking the attentional window. *Acta Psychologica* 135(2):77–99 — [PMC2948621](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2948621/); rebuttal Egeth, Leonard & Leber (2010) *Acta Psychologica* "Why salience is not enough" — [asc.ohio-state.edu](https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/psychology/cclab/downloads/Egeth2010.pdf)
- Sawaki & Luck (2010), Primary (ERP). Irrelevant colour singletons elicited no N2pc (no capture) but a Pd (suppression) component, equally inside and outside the attended region: "salient singletons may automatically produce an attend-to-me signal, irrespective of top-down control settings, but this signal can be overridden by an active suppression process." *AP&P* 72(6):1455–1470, PMID 20675793 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/APP.72.6.1455)
- Gaspelin, Leonard & Luck (2015), Primary. Capture-probe paradigm: probe report at the singleton location fell BELOW that at non-singleton distractor locations when conditions avoided capture — direct behavioural evidence of suppression below baseline. *Psychological Science* 26(11):1740–1750, PMID 26420441 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615597913)
- Gaspelin & Luck (2018), Review. Signal suppression hypothesis: "salient stimuli naturally attempt to capture attention, but capture can be avoided if the salient stimulus is suppressed before it captures attention"; converging psychophysics, eye-tracking (first saccades land on the singleton LESS often than on average non-singletons) and ERP (Pd within ~150 ms). *TiCS* 22(1):79–92, PMID 29191511 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2017.11.001)
- Luck et al. (2021), Review/consensus. Agreed statements: (1) "Certain kinds of stimuli (e.g., abrupt onsets, color singletons) automatically generate a priority signal that, in the absence of specific attentional control settings, will automatically capture attention." (2) "The capture of attention by salient singleton stimuli can be prevented if the attentional control system is appropriately configured." Disputed: set-size dependence (Wang & Theeuwes 2020 found suppression at set size 4 but not 6–10); whether suppression is proactive feature-gain modulation (Gaspelin & Luck) or only reactive/spatial (Theeuwes); whether explicit instruction suffices ("participants cannot suppress a singleton simply by being explicitly told its color in advance; instead, suppression builds up over several trials"). Consensus Figure 2: feature maps → saliency computation → priority map, with goals and selection history acting at the priority map (agreed) and proactive feature suppression (disputed). *Visual Cognition* 29(1):1–21 — [PMC7872136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7872136)
- Lamy (2021), Commentary. Argues the consensus paper "glosses over important differences" and that conclusions rest on net capture/suppression effects rather than their modulation by relevant variables. *Visual Cognition* 29(9):544–547 — [TAU record](https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/the-attentional-capture-debate-the-long-lasting-consequences-of-a/)
- Theeuwes (2023), Review. Concedes capture is avoidable when the target is non-salient, search is (partly) serial with a narrowed attentional window, set sizes are small (4–6) or displays heterogeneous; "salient signals outside the focused attentional window do not capture attention anymore not because they are suppressed but because they are ignored." When the target is salient and search is parallel, "any salient distractor will capture attention"; capture effects of ~65–80 ms under parallel search with large displays (citing Theeuwes 2004) vs none in serial search. *Journal of Cognition* 6(1):35 — [PMC10327859](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10327859/)

### Inferences
- "Unconditional vs contingent" is a false dichotomy in current theory: the priority signal is unconditional, the ORIENTING is conditional on (a) whether the item falls inside the current attentional window, (b) whether a learned feature-suppression setting exists, and (c) search mode. A design that computes a salience term always but gates its effect on orienting through a learned mask is exactly the consensus architecture.
- Suppression settings are LEARNED over trials, not instructed — maps onto "priming/selection history feeds the mask" rather than a declarative rule.

### Gaps
- Raw RT costs from Theeuwes (1992) itself were not retrieved; the 65–80 ms figure is Theeuwes' own citation of Theeuwes (2004) in the 2023 paper.
- Wang & Theeuwes (2020) was not independently verified (cited inside Luck et al. 2021).

## KQ3. Surprise capture: Horstmann's program, eye-movement evidence, Itti & Baldi's Bayesian surprise, and latency

### Takeaway
An expectancy-discrepant feature on its first unannounced appearance does capture attention and gaze, independently of the attentional set and independently of singleton status, but with a characteristic ~400 ms engagement latency (second fixation onward), it habituates after 1–2 exposures, and at least one well-known study (Gibson & Jiang 1998) found no capture; computationally, Bayesian surprise (KL divergence prior→posterior) predicts human gaze better than feature-contrast saliency, outlier detection or entropy.

### Cited Findings
- Horstmann (2002), Primary. Letter search; an unannounced colour singleton presented 500 ms before the letters gave accuracy equal to a feature-search condition (singleton always at target) and better than conjunction search; NO improvement when the singleton appeared simultaneously with the letters (capture occurs later than in feature search); Exp. 3: RT independent of set size on the surprise trial. "Surprising color singletons capture attention independently of a corresponding attentional set." *Psychological Science* 13(6):499–505, PMID 12430832 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00488)
- Horstmann (2005), Primary. Set-size logic: "attentional capture by an unannounced color singleton was due to a mismatch with expectations concerning the color of the object and not due to its being a singleton." *JEP:HPP* 31(5):1039–1060, PMID 16262497 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.31.5.1039)
- Horstmann (2006), Primary. Time course: "accuracy with expected singletons was barely affected by SOA, whereas SOA strongly affected accuracy with the unexpected singleton" — unexpected capture is delayed relative to set-matching capture. *Psychological Research* 70(1):13–25, PMID 15340848 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-004-0184-7)
- Horstmann & Ansorge (2006), Primary. Rare singletons (4% of trials) produced large accuracy benefits and search-efficiency gains; the pattern was judged "consistent with the contingent-capture view." *Visual Cognition* 14(3):295–325 — [Bielefeld record](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/1598857) (journal not PubMed-indexed; verified via university repositories)
- Becker & Horstmann (2011), Primary. "A motion singleton only captured attention on its first unannounced occurrence when the observers had not encountered moving items before in the experiment, whereas it failed to capture when observers were familiar with moving items" — novelty of the feature class, not salience, drives surprise capture. *Acta Psychologica* 136(3):290–299, PMID 21208608 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2010.12.002)
- Horstmann & Herwig (2015), Primary (eye tracking). On the unannounced first presentation of a novel colour, "the novel color was quickly responded to with an eye movement, and ... gaze was not turned away" for a considerable time — surprise both attracts and binds gaze. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 22(3):743–749, PMID 25199467 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0723-1)
- Horstmann & Herwig (2016), Primary (eye tracking). Novel colour prioritised and familiar colour DE-prioritised, "clearly present at the second fixation, and spans over the next couple of fixations. Saliency, on the other hand, did not prioritize items in the display." Frames novelty as a third category beyond task-driven and stimulus-driven: "the contrast or discrepancy between active representations and the upcoming stimulus." *AP&P* 78(1):69–77, PMID 26486643 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-015-0995-1)
- Horstmann, Becker & Ernst (2016), Primary. Perceptual salience does capture the eyes on a surprise trial, "however, contrary to predictions of prominent neuro-computational and psychological salience models, prioritization was not fast-acting." *AP&P* 78(7):1889–1900, PMID 27250362 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-016-1102-y)
- Horstmann (2015), Review. Surprise = "expectancy discrepant events"; "if the expectancy discrepancy can be determined on the basis of simple, preattentively available information, attention can be captured by the surprising stimulus"; the mechanism "constantly tests expectancies on different levels of representation"; "Evidence also converges on a latency of an attentional engagement of nearly 400 ms. This seems to be a unique feature of surprise capture that also questions the validity of models proposing that saliency is an early automatic attractor of attention." *Annals NYAS* 1339:106–115, PMID 25682693 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.12679)
- Retell, Venini & Becker (2015), Primary (eye tracking). "Strong evidence of surprise capture for the first presentation of an unannounced color singleton" in both unguided and guided search, but eye movements contradicted the delayed-onset account: costs appeared only AFTER target selection → an interference account of surprise capture. *AP&P* 77(5):1529–1543, PMID 25832190 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-015-0888-3)
- Gibson & Jiang (1998), Primary — the main counter-result. "An initial and unexpected encounter with a color singleton" did NOT capture attention in visual search; efficient search for the singleton emerged only after its relevance was established. *Psychological Science* 9(3):176–182 — [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/surprise-an-unexpected-color-singleton-does-not-capture-attention/) (journal page [psychologicalscience.org](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/1467-9280.00034/))
- Asplund, Todd, Snyder, Gilbert & Marois (2010), Primary. Surprise-induced blindness: a novel, unexpected, task-irrelevant stimulus in an RSVP stream "virtually abolishes conscious detection of a target presented within half a second after the 'Surprise' stimulus, but only for its earliest occurrences (generally 1 to 2 presentations)" — contrasted with the milder, enduring capture by ordinary singletons. *JEP:HPP* 36(6):1372–1381, PMID 20919779 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020551)
- Reisenzein, Horstmann & Schützwohl (2019), Review. Cognitive-evolutionary model: "Surprise is evoked by unexpected (schema-discrepant) events and its intensity is determined by the degree of schema-discrepancy, whereas the novelty and the valence of the eliciting events probably do not have an independent effect." *Topics in Cognitive Science* 11(1):50–74, PMID 28940761 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12292)
- Itti & Baldi (2009), Primary + formalisation. Surprise S(D,M) = KL(P(M|D) ‖ P(M)) — the divergence between posterior and prior beliefs over models; unit "wow" (1 wow = 1 bit shift, log base 2); implemented with Poisson/Gamma models attached to 72 low-level feature detectors at each location, yielding local temporal surprise (generalising outlier-based novelty) and spatial surprise (generalising outlier-based saliency). "Only data observations which substantially affect the observer's beliefs yield surprise, irrespectively of how rare or informative in Shannon's sense these observations are." Snow paradox: TV clips carried 50.83 ± 0.43 wows/s vs 2.99 ± 0.02 for random snow (17:1) while Shannon information was 0.25 vs 4.90 MB/s (1:20). Test: 8 observers, 50 clips, >25 min, 10,192 saccades (4 observers per clip). Human-vs-random KL scores: surprise 0.241 ± 0.006 > saliency 0.205 ± 0.006 ≈ outliers 0.204 ± 0.006 > motion 0.180 > flicker 0.179 > entropy 0.151 > static contrast metrics 0.07–0.10; surprise "nearly 20% better than the second-best metric (saliency) and 60% better than the best static metric (entropy)". "Over 72% of all human saccades were targeted to locations predicted to be more surprising than on average"; 76/80/84% when 2/3/4 observers agreed. Authors note surprise "quickly becomes unsurprising" for a shower of random pixels that would keep exciting outlier detectors. *Vision Research* 49(10):1295–1306 — [ilab.usc.edu](https://ilab.usc.edu/publications/Itti_Baldi09vr.html)

### Inferences
- Surprise is a priority source distinct from feature-contrast salience along three axes: (i) it needs a learned prior (first-presentation only; Becker & Horstmann; Asplund), (ii) it is slow (~400 ms; second fixation), (iii) it binds dwell time (Horstmann & Herwig 2015). Feature salience is fast but suppressible; surprise is slow and (so far) not shown to be suppressible by instruction.
- A subtractive "predicted image minus arrival" residual is a prediction-error quantity, not Itti–Baldi surprise (KL over beliefs). The two diverge exactly in the snow paradox: persistent high residuals that fail to move beliefs are NOT surprising. If the design wants habituation (as in Asplund; Becker & Horstmann) the novelty term should be belief-shift-like, e.g. a magnitude of prior update, rather than raw residual energy.
- Horstmann's "expectancies on different levels of representation" is the same multi-level structure as "a predicted image per level"; the literature supports computing the discrepancy per level and feeding whichever level is preattentively available.

### Gaps
- Horstmann (2002) numeric accuracy gains (percent correct per condition) were not retrieved — abstract only.
- Full text of Horstmann (2015) was blocked (Bielefeld anti-bot); the 400 ms figure is from the abstract.
- No direct test found of whether surprise capture can be proactively suppressed (the Gaspelin–Luck question applied to surprise).

## KQ4. Inattentional blindness: what is missed, what is noticed, and the roles of attentional set, meaning, capacity and expertise

### Takeaway
Roughly a third to a half of observers miss a fully visible unexpected object even when fixating it; noticing is governed primarily by similarity to the current attentional set (visual or semantic), secondarily by expectancy discrepancy, with faces as a robust and own-name/familiar-symbols/spiders as weak or contested exceptions; stable individual differences and expertise contribute little, and the newest large-N work shows "blind" observers retain above-chance feature sensitivity, i.e. IB is partly a conservative report criterion rather than total absence of processing.

### Cited Findings
- Mack & Rock (1998), *Inattentional Blindness*, MIT Press (book; Primary program). As summarised in Simons & Chabris (1999, Section 1.1): "(i) About 25% of subjects are inattentionally blind when the cross is presented at fixation and the unexpected object is presented parafoveally ... (ii) About 75% of subjects are inattentionally blind when the cross is presented parafoveally and the unexpected object is presented at fixation ... (iii) These levels of detection are no different for features thought to be preattentively processed (eg color, orientation, motion) ... (iv) ... Observers typically notice their own name or a smiley face even when they did not expect it. Note, however, they do not tend to notice their own name if one letter is changed ... (v) ... Objects that appear inside this zone of attention are more likely to be detected." — [Simons & Chabris 1999 PDF](http://www.chabris.com/Simons1999.pdf)
- Ding, Simons, Hults & Raja (2024), Primary (4 preregistered experiments, N = 1,700), on Mack & Rock's meaning effects: own name vs modified name ("Jack" vs "Jeck") — "Both studies tested small numbers of participants, and the difference was not statistically significant"; "slightly more noticing of the word 'The' than the less common word 'Tie'"; happy face noticed more than sad/neutral/inverted/scrambled. New data: upright schematic faces noticed ~67% vs inverted/scrambled ~50% (no happy-face advantage); familiar vs unfamiliar flags/logos showed no consistent difference (Italy flag ~50% vs South Africa ~48%; logos ~52 vs 49%, and ~37 vs 45%). Conclusion: familiarity with arbitrary symbols does not reduce IB; faces may be an exception. *Journal of Cognition* 7(1):28 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.352)
- Simons & Chabris (1999), Primary. N = 192, 16 conditions, unexpected event visible 5 s. Overall 54% noticed / 46% missed. Opaque video 67% noticed vs Transparent 42%; Easy task 64% vs Hard 45%; Umbrella Woman 65% vs Gorilla 44%. Gorilla noticed by 58% attending the BLACK team vs 27% attending the WHITE team (p = .002); Umbrella Woman unaffected by team (62% vs 69%). Table 1 extremes: Transparent/Gorilla/White team 8% (Easy and Hard) vs Opaque/Umbrella/White/Easy 100%; Opaque/Gorilla/White/Easy 42%, Black/Easy 83%. A 9-s chest-thumping gorilla (n = 12) was noticed by 50%. *Perception* 28(9):1059–1074, PMID 10694957 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059)
- Most, Simons, Scholl, Jimenez, Clifford & Chabris (2001), Primary. "The more similar an unexpected object is to the attended items, and the greater its difference from the ignored items, the more likely it is that people will notice it"; Exp. 3: "nearly 30% of observers failed to notice a bright red cross move across the display, even though it had a unique color, luminance, shape, and motion trajectory and was visible for 5s." *Psychological Science* 12(1):9–17, PMID 11294235 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00303)
- Most, Scholl, Clifford & Simons (2005), Review + Primary. "What you see is what you set": attentional goals are "the primary determinant of whether people notice unexpected stimuli, despite other stimulus properties also playing a role"; "many—but not all—aspects of attention capture apply to inattentional blindness but ... these 2 classes of phenomena remain importantly distinct." *Psychological Review* 112(1):217–242, PMID 15631594 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.112.1.217)
- Simons (2000), Review. Distinguishes implicit capture (performance effects of irrelevant stimuli one expects) from explicit capture (awareness of unexpected stimuli); "quite often, unexpected objects fail to capture attention." *TiCS* 4(4):147–155, PMID 10740279 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1364-6613(00)01455-8)
- Koivisto & Revonsuo (2007), Primary. "An unexpected stimulus belonging to the attended semantic category but not sharing physical features with the attended stimuli was detected more often than a semantically unrelated stimulus. This effect was larger when the unexpected stimuli were words than when they were pictures ... Meaning may determine whether or not one sees a stimulus." *Psychological Science* 18(10):845–849, PMID 17894599 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01989.x)
- Horstmann & Ansorge (2016), Primary. With salience excluded (two objects per display), "an expectancy discrepant novel feature is noticed even if it is completely dissimilar to the target" (Exp. 1); expectancy discrepancy and target-match prioritise simultaneously (Exp. 2); "IB is related to the degree of expectancy discrepancy" (Exp. 3). "IB is prevented by two factors: match to target set and expectancy discrepancy." *Cognition* 157:237–249, PMID 27665396 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2016.09.005)
- Devue, Laloyaux, Feyers, Theeuwes & Brédart (2009), Primary. Face pictures "resist more to inattentional blindness than pictures of common objects or inverted faces", but "no differential resistance to blindness" among own face, friend's face and unknown face — not familiarity/identity driven. *Perception* 38(4):552–568, PMID 19522323 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1068/p6049)
- Qiu, Lei, Becker & Pegna (2023), Primary (EEG). N2pc to faces "only when participants were aware of the faces and when they were task-relevant"; "fearful faces, or faces in general, do not attract attention unless we want them to." *Biological Psychology* 183:108665, PMID 37619811 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2023.108665)
- New & German (2015), Primary. Static cross task; Exp. 1 N = 252, Exp. 2 N = 320. Prototypical spider detected by 81% (Exp. 1) and 80% (Exp. 2), localised ~75%/68%, identified ~72%/63%, full report (all three) 53%/53%; hypodermic needle detected 53%/53%, identified ~8% in Exp. 2; housefly detected 73% but identified ~10% (Exp. 2). Fear ratings for spiders or needles did not predict detection, localisation or identification (all p > .14). Conclusion: an evolutionarily persistent threat is "uniquely capable of capturing observers' visual attention and awareness." *Evolution and Human Behavior* 36(3):165–173, DOI 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2014.08.004 (Crossref-verified) — [UCSB PDF](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/german/tamsin/uploads/2/4/0/4/24040853/10.1.1.708.4662.pdf)
- Wiemer, Gerdes & Pauli (2013), Primary — conflicting. IB task with N = 120: "reported detection rates were not enhanced for spiders" vs flowers and spider fear had no moderating effect, although spiders produced larger skin-conductance responses and were "initially fixated more often" — arousal and early orienting without awareness. *Psychological Research* 77(2):155–166, PMID 22227916 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-011-0407-7)
- Seegmiller, Watson & Strayer (2011), Primary. Gorilla video: 58% noticed overall; among accurate counters, high-WMC 67% vs low-WMC 36% noticed. *JEP:LMC* 37(3):785–791, PMID 21299325 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022474)
- Kreitz, Furley, Memmert & Simons (2015), Primary — conflicting. Static and dynamic, central and spatial IB tasks: "none of the cognitive measures predicted noticing in the dynamic central task or in either the static or dynamic spatial task. Only in the central static task did working memory capacity predict noticing, and that relationship was fairly weak"; noticing "driven more by stochastic processes common to all people than by stable individual differences." *PLoS ONE* 10(8):e0134675, PMID 26258545 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0134675)
- Memmert (2006), Primary. Children who missed the unexpected object "spent on average as much time (about one second) looking at the unexpected object as those subjects who did perceive it"; basketball experts noticed significantly more than novices. *Consciousness and Cognition* 15(3):620–627, PMID 16487725 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2006.01.001)
- Drew, Võ & Wolfe (2013), Primary. 24 radiologists, lung-nodule search, gorilla 48× nodule size in the last case: 83% did not see it; "the majority of those who missed the gorilla looked directly at its location." *Psychological Science* 24(9):1848–1853, PMID 23863753 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386)
- Ekelund, Fernsund, Karlsson & Mac Giolla (2022), Meta-analysis. K = 14, N = 1,153: IB in 62% of novices vs 56% of experts, weighted OR 1.33, 95% CI [0.78, 2.28]; domain relevance of the stimulus had no moderating effect. *Perception* 51(2):131–147, PMID 35060780 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/03010066211072466)
- Nartker, Firestone, Egeth & Phillips (2025), Primary (largest IB dataset, >25,000 subjects; Exp. 5 alone 10,830 after exclusions). Mack & Rock-style cross: 28.6% non-noticers (Exp. 1), yet 63.6% of them chose the correct 2AFC location (d′ = 0.51); colour d′ = 0.38 (Exp. 2); high-confidence non-noticers still d′ = 0.34 (Exp. 3, N = 204). Absent trials: only 7.2% false alarms → conservative criterion c = 0.31 (Exp. 2), c = 0.85 (Exp. 4), c = 0.45 (Exp. 5). Most-style dynamic display (5 s): 57.3% IB (Exp. 4); non-noticers above chance for colour (d′ = 0.82; 0.12 in the debiased Exp. 5) and shape (d′ = 0.21; 0.23) but not location. "Perceptual information remains accessible in IB"; observers are "essentially 'playing it safe'". *eLife* 13:RP100337, PMID 40388213 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.100337)

### Inferences
- The noticing hierarchy from strongest to weakest evidence: (1) match to the current attentional set, visual or semantic (Most 2001/2005; Koivisto & Revonsuo); (2) expectancy discrepancy of a preattentive feature (Horstmann & Ansorge 2016); (3) faces as a category (Devue; Ding; but task-relevance-gated at the N2pc level per Qiu 2023); (4) own name / familiar symbols — weak, non-significant or null in large preregistered samples; (5) ancestral threat — split (New & German vs Wiemer).
- Capacity and expertise are poor predictors (Kreitz; Ekelund), so IB is better modelled as a stochastic gate set by the attentional set than as a resource-limited one — except under perceptual load (KQ5).
- Nartker et al. imply that "unattended" items leave a weak but real trace (location/colour/shape) in the field even when not admitted to report — consistent with a graded mask rather than a hard zero outside attention.

### Gaps
- Exact Mack & Rock own-name noticing percentages were not retrieved (book not accessible; secondary sources agree the own-name vs altered-name difference was non-significant).
- New & German's localisation/identification percentages for needles in Exp. 1 are ambiguous in the extracted PDF layout; detection (53%) and spider numbers are clear.
- Most et al. (2005) per-condition numbers not retrieved (abstract only).

## KQ5. Load theory: perceptual load as the gate on processing of unattended items

### Takeaway
Under low perceptual load, even entirely task-irrelevant distractors (a cartoon figure) intrude and slow responses; raising perceptual load eliminates both distractor interference and awareness of unexpected objects (more IB), with correlates down to V1 — i.e. novelty/salience terms should only win when the attended task leaves spare capacity.

### Cited Findings
- Lavie (1995), Primary. Response-competition paradigm; load manipulated by set size or by processing requirement (feature vs conjunction; detection vs size/position identification): "Distractors' interference was found only under low-load conditions ... physical separation is not a sufficient condition for selective perception; overloading perception is also required." *JEP:HPP* 21(3):451–468, PMID 7790827 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037//0096-1523.21.3.451)
- Lavie (2005), Review. "High perceptual load can eliminate distractor processing, high load on 'frontal' cognitive control processes increases distractor processing." *TiCS* 9(2):75–82, PMID 15668100 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2004.12.004)
- Cartwright-Finch & Lavie (2007), Primary. IB paradigm: "awareness of a task-irrelevant stimulus was significantly reduced by higher perceptual load (with increased numbers of search items, or a harder discrimination vs. detection task)" — conscious perception of irrelevant stimuli "critically depends upon the level of task-relevant perceptual load rather than intentions or expectations." *Cognition* 102(3):321–340, PMID 16480973 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2006.01.002)
- Forster & Lavie (2008), Primary. "An entirely irrelevant distractor can interfere with task performance to the same extent as a response-competing distractor and ... the interfering effects of the irrelevant distractors can be eliminated with high perceptual load." *JEP:Applied* 14(1):73–83, PMID 18377168 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898x.14.1.73)
- Lavie, Beck & Konstantinou (2014), Review. "Although distractors will intrude upon awareness in conditions of low load, awareness will be restricted to the content of focused attention when the attended information involves high perceptual load"; reviews load-induced reductions of IB/change detection, of d′ for peripheral stimuli, of V1/LGN responses and of the early C1 ERP; reports a rightward shift of the contrast-response function under load (detection threshold ~18% → ~27% contrast, reduced contrast gain). On exceptions: faces, scene gist and animals "sometimes show reduced inattentional blindness", explained as lower thresholds for inherently primed categories, "however, sufficiently high load should still impair awareness of even socio-biologically significant stimuli." *Phil Trans R Soc B* 369(1641):20130205 — [PMC3965161](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3965161/)
- Murphy, Groeger & Greene (2016), Review. Twenty-year appraisal: evidence for perceptual-load and cognitive-load effects, individual differences, alternative accounts (dilution etc.) and criticisms; argues the next step is real-world application. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 23(5):1316–1340, PMID 26728138 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0982-5)

### Inferences
- Load supplies the capacity side of the gate: salience/novelty outside the attended set is processed and intrudes only when the attended task leaves spare capacity. For a serial reader this predicts that unexpected tokens are more likely to be admitted during easy, predictable stretches than during demanding ones.
- Load theory and the attentional-set account are complementary, not rival: set determines WHAT escapes IB, load determines WHETHER anything does.

### Gaps
- Exact IB percentages under low vs high load in Cartwright-Finch & Lavie (2007) were not retrieved (abstract only).
- Attribution of the 18%/27% contrast thresholds to a specific primary study inside the 2014 review is uncertain from the retrieved summary.

## KQ6. Selection history and priming as a third factor; components of the priority map

### Takeaway
Current models replace the top-down/bottom-up dichotomy with an integrated priority map fed by (at least) bottom-up salience, top-down feature guidance, selection history/priming, value/reward and scene structure; novelty is not listed as a separate source but enters through history (habituation) and, in Schomaker & Meeter's analysis, through a transient deviance/arousal route distinct from longer-lasting novelty effects.

### Cited Findings
- Maljkovic & Nakayama (1994), Primary. Priming of pop-out: colour-singleton search is faster when the target colour repeats; the implicit trace persists ~5–8 trials. *Memory & Cognition* 22(6):657–672 — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt65x4v69b/qt65x4v69b.pdf)
- Awh, Belopolsky & Theeuwes (2012), Review. The dichotomy "fails to explain a growing number of cases in which neither current goals nor physical salience can account for strong selection biases. For example, equally salient stimuli associated with reward can capture attention, even when this contradicts current selection goals ... We describe an alternative framework, in which past selection history is integrated with current goals and physical salience to shape an integrated priority map." *TiCS* 16(8):437–443, PMID 22795563 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2012.06.010)
- Wolfe & Horowitz (2017), Review. Five guiding factors: "Bottom-up salience, top-down feature guidance, scene structure and meaning, the previous history of search over time scales from msec to years, and the relative value of the targets and distractors." *Nature Human Behaviour* 1:0058, PMID 36711068 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0058)
- Wolfe (2021), Review/model. GS6: guidance from "(1) top-down and (2) bottom-up feature guidance, (3) prior history (e.g., priming), (4) reward, and (5) scene syntax and semantics", combined into a spatial priority map that selects ~20 items/s while recognition is a >150 ms diffusion per item (hybrid serial/parallel). *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 28(4):1060–1092, PMID 33547630 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-020-01859-9)
- Schomaker & Meeter (2015), Review. Three groups of novelty effects: (1) transient perceptual enhancement via amygdala; (2) arousal-driven effects on action "in the first hundreds of milliseconds after presentation ... related to deviance, rather than to novelty per se" and linked to the locus coeruleus–norepinephrine system; (3) spatial novelty → dopaminergic mesolimbic system, effects "up to tens of minutes" on motivation and learning. *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* 55:268–279, PMID 25976634 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2015.05.002)
- Benoni & Ressler (2020), Commentary. Argues the trichotomy terminology (top-down/bottom-up/selection history) should itself be reconsidered as a spectrum. *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:2243, PMID 33101107 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.02243)
- Luck et al. (2021), Review. Selection history (reward and distractor-location learning) "shapes attentional control offline, independently of explicit goals"; Folk & Remington note it may operate through separate neural systems from goal-driven control. — [PMC7872136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7872136)

### Inferences
- A diffuse, unlocated concept-level prior feeding the mask corresponds to the "history/priming" and "scene semantics" channels of GS6, both of which are legitimate priority-map inputs and both of which are learned rather than instructed.
- Schomaker & Meeter's split suggests two timescales for a novelty term: a fast deviance/arousal transient (hundreds of ms, LC-NE-like, feeds orienting now) and a slow novelty/exploration signal (minutes, dopaminergic, feeds learning/motivation) — these should probably be separate quantities in a design.

### Gaps
- Maljkovic & Nakayama abstract text was not retrieved from a primary metadata source (Memory & Cognition 1994 not in Europe PMC); the citation is well established and the PDF is linked.

## KQ7. Is novelty itself (a never-seen item) a salience signal distinct from feature-contrast salience?

### Takeaway
Yes, in a qualified sense: novelty produces "novel popout" and search asymmetries when the FAMILIAR items can be processed fluently and the novel one cannot, it captures on first appearance of a feature class and then habituates, and it de-prioritises familiar items; but it is slower than feature contrast, depends on what has been learned, and some surprise paradigms find no capture at all.

### Cited Findings
- Johnston, Hawley, Plewe, Elliott & DeWitt (1990), Primary. Brief 4-word arrays, localisation accuracy; words seen many times (familiar) vs once (novel). Not intermixed (0:4, 4:0): familiar localised better. Intermixed, especially 1:3: "accuracy tended to be higher for the novel words. This novel popout effect was the outcome of the suppressed localizability of the familiar words ... and the enhanced localizability of the novel words ... Novel popout may reflect an automatic orientation of attention away from more fluently unfolding regions of the perceptual field (familiar objects) and toward less fluently unfolding regions (novel objects)." *JEP:General* 119(4):397–411, PMID 2148574 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037//0096-3445.119.4.397)
- Wang, Cavanagh & Green (1994), Primary. Letters vs mirror-image letters (same low-level features): "familiarity speeds visual search ... principally when the distractors, not the targets, are familiar"; slopes from ~1.5 ms/item (novel target among familiar distractors) to ~82 ms/item depending on familiarity. *Perception & Psychophysics* 56(5):495–500, PMID 7991347 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03206946)
- Becker & Horstmann (2011), Primary. Motion singleton captured only on its first unannounced appearance and only if no moving items had been seen before in the experiment — novelty of the feature class, not motion salience, drives the capture. *Acta Psychologica* 136(3):290–299, PMID 21208608 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2010.12.002)
- Horstmann & Herwig (2016), Primary. Novel colour prioritised, familiar colour de-prioritised from the second fixation on; "Saliency, on the other hand, did not prioritize items in the display." *AP&P* 78(1):69–77, PMID 26486643 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-015-0995-1)
- Neo & Chua (2006) and Folk & Remington (2015), Primary (see KQ1). Rarity restores onset capture under focused attention and lets onsets override a colour set; Folk & Remington attribute this to rapidly habituating novelty mechanisms (Luck et al. 2021). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03193728); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000084)
- Asplund et al. (2010), Primary. Surprise-induced blindness is strongest for the first 1–2 presentations of a novel stimulus. *JEP:HPP* 36(6):1372–1381, PMID 20919779 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020551)
- Gibson & Jiang (1998), Primary — null. A first, unexpected colour singleton did not capture attention in search. *Psychological Science* 9(3):176–182 — [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/surprise-an-unexpected-color-singleton-does-not-capture-attention/)
- Ding et al. (2024), Primary — null for FAMILIARITY in IB: familiar flags/logos no more noticed than unfamiliar ones. *Journal of Cognition* 7(1):28 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.352)
- Itti & Baldi (2009), formalisation. Temporal surprise generalises outlier-based "novelty" and spatial surprise generalises outlier-based "saliency"; the surprise metric with the identical front end beat the outlier metric (KL 0.241 vs 0.204), so the gain "must reflect a difference between computing outliers and computing surprise." *Vision Research* 49(10):1295–1306 — [ilab.usc.edu](https://ilab.usc.edu/publications/Itti_Baldi09vr.html)
- Reisenzein, Horstmann & Schützwohl (2019), Review. Surprise intensity is set by schema discrepancy; "the novelty and the valence of the eliciting events probably do not have an independent effect." *Topics in Cognitive Science* 11(1):50–74, PMID 28940761 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12292)

### Inferences
- Novelty operates as a relational quantity (novel-among-familiar), not an absolute property: Johnston's and Wang's effects vanish when everything is novel. This is a contrast computed over LEARNED fluency/priors rather than over feature values — exactly a low-prior (unprimed) item amid primed ones.
- The two negative results (Gibson & Jiang; Ding et al.) and the ~400 ms latency argue against treating novelty as a fast, unconditional salience term equal in status to onsets.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly pits a never-seen item against a feature-contrast singleton while equating both for expectancy discrepancy; Horstmann & Herwig (2016) and Horstmann, Becker & Ernst (2016) are the closest.

## KQ8. Synthesis for the design question: should low-prior (novel/unexpected) unattended items capture attention, and under which conditions?

### Takeaway
The literature supports a novelty/surprise term as a priority-map input, but as a THIRD channel with its own dynamics: slower than onset/salience, computed from a learned prior per level, habituating after one or two exposures, gated by perceptual load, and competing with — not overriding — match to the current attentional set, which remains the strongest determinant of what escapes inattentional blindness.

### Cited Findings
- Automatic priority signal + suppressibility is the consensus (Luck et al. 2021) — [PMC7872136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7872136)
- Onsets/new objects are the exception that resists suppression and overrides set when rare (Yantis & Hillstrom 1994; Folk & Remington 2015; Theeuwes 2023) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037//0096-1523.20.1.95); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000084); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.251)
- Surprise capture is set-independent but ~400 ms and first-presentation-bound (Horstmann 2002, 2015; Becker & Horstmann 2011; Asplund et al. 2010) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00488); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.12679); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2010.12.002); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020551)
- IB is prevented by two factors, target-set match and expectancy discrepancy (Horstmann & Ansorge 2016); semantic match suffices (Koivisto & Revonsuo 2007); even a unique red cross is missed by ~30% (Most et al. 2001); the gorilla by 46% (Simons & Chabris 1999) and by 83% of radiologists (Drew et al. 2013) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2016.09.005); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01989.x); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00303); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386)
- High perceptual load removes both irrelevant-distractor interference and awareness of unexpected items (Forster & Lavie 2008; Cartwright-Finch & Lavie 2007; Lavie et al. 2014) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898x.14.1.73); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2006.01.002); [PMC3965161](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3965161/)
- Bayesian surprise (KL prior→posterior) is the best-validated single formalisation of a novelty-as-salience quantity and is explicitly distinct from both feature-contrast saliency and Shannon information (Itti & Baldi 2009) — [ilab.usc.edu](https://ilab.usc.edu/publications/Itti_Baldi09vr.html)
- "Blind" observers retain above-chance feature sensitivity; IB reports are criterion-conservative (Nartker et al. 2025) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.100337)

### Inferences
- Answer to "unconditional, contingent, or load-dependent": all three, layered. (1) New-object/onset arrivals: near-unconditional priority, blocked only by a tightly focused set, boosted by rarity. (2) Feature-contrast salience: always computed, but orienting is contingent on learned suppression settings and search mode. (3) Novelty/surprise: contingent on a learned prior, slow, habituating, not shown to be instruction-suppressible, and effective in IB only alongside set-match. (4) All of (1)–(3) are gated by spare perceptual capacity.
- For the mask: a novelty term derived from the diffuse concept prior (low priming = high surprise) is well motivated, but it should (a) be a belief-shift quantity that decays with repeated exposure rather than a persistent residual, (b) enter the priority map later than the onset term (two-stage latency), and (c) lose to a strong set-match term, since set-match is the dominant predictor of noticing. Items with high surprise AND zero set-match should still be missed a sizeable fraction of the time (≈30–50% in human data), which argues for a stochastic rather than deterministic admission.
- What gets through under inattention is primarily what matches the current set (visually or semantically), then expectancy-discrepant simple features, then faces; own name and familiar symbols are weak. A text reader's analogue of "faces" would be a small set of overlearned, category-level detectors; "own name" effects do not justify a general familiarity bonus.

### Gaps
- No study was found that manipulates novelty of an unattended item in a SERIAL reading / RSVP word stream with inattentional-blindness outcome measures; the closest are Asplund et al. (2010, RSVP) and Folk & Remington (2015, RSVP), both with visual onsets rather than lexical novelty.
- Whether surprise capture can be proactively suppressed (the signal-suppression question) is untested in the retrieved literature.
