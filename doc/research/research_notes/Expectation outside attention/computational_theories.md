# Computational and theoretical accounts of how attention relates to expectation/prediction

Scope: predictive coding and attention-as-precision; active inference's attention/salience split; Bayesian theories of attention; classical theories (filter, attenuation, FIT, biased competition, normalization, TVA, Guided Search); ML hard attention and its credit-assignment problem; novelty/curiosity bonuses in ML. Every citation below was checked against a publisher, repository, or arXiv record during this research pass (2026-10-09). Items that could not be confirmed are marked UNVERIFIED. Quotations are verbatim from the fetched text unless marked "as summarised".

Conventions used below: "prediction everywhere" = every channel/location carries a predicted value and a prediction error regardless of attention; "prediction in-region" = predictions/errors are formed only for attended content.

## KQ1. Predictive coding: is prediction made everywhere with attention weighting the error (precision), or only within the attended region? Which theories commit to which, on what evidence, and who objects?

### Takeaway
Every predictive-coding/free-energy formulation examined (Rao & Ballard 1999; Spratling 2008; Feldman & Friston 2010; Hohwy 2012; Clark 2013; Kanai et al. 2015) commits to **prediction everywhere**: prediction errors ε are formed at every unit in the hierarchy and attention is a *second-order* quantity, the expected precision Π that multiplies them (ξ = Π ε) by changing the synaptic gain of error units. No predictive-coding theory restricts prediction to the attended region; the "in-region" effect is produced by low gain outside the attended set, not by absent predictions. The best controlled evidence (Kok et al. 2012; Jiang et al. 2013; Auksztulewicz & Friston 2015; Smout et al. 2019) shows prediction-error signals exist for unattended stimuli but are weaker and that attention *enhances* the expected/unexpected difference (PE-promotion), while critics argue that precision alone cannot explain capture by rare/irrelevant events (Bowman et al. 2013; Ransom & Fazelpour 2015; Ransom, Fazelpour & Mole 2017), that "expectation suppression" may be reduced attention in disguise (Alink & Blank 2021), and that expectation may act on decision rather than early sensory stages when relevance is controlled (Rungratsameetaweemana & Serences 2019).

### Cited Findings

**The formal commitment: attention = expected precision of prediction errors**

- Rao & Ballard (1999), *Nature Neuroscience* 2(1):79–87, "Predictive coding in the visual cortex: a functional interpretation of some extra-classical receptive-field effects": "feedback connections from a higher- to a lower-order visual cortical area carry predictions of lower-level neural activities, whereas the feedforward connections carry the residual errors between the predictions and the actual lower-level activities"; the error-carrying units developed endstopping and other extra-classical surround effects when trained on natural images. No attention term is present; prediction and error exist at every unit — [Caltech Authors record](https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:20130816-103208185); [Nature Neurosci abstract listing](https://www.qigonginstitute.org/abstract-print/17604)
- Spratling (2008), *Vision Research* 48(12):1391–1408, "Predictive coding as a model of biased competition in visual attention": the abstract notes that attention "enhance[s] the response of cells encoding expected or predicted information" whereas predictive coding "proposes that feedback acts to suppress information predicted by higher-level cortical regions". Spratling shows the two are one algorithm: with error units e^{S_i} = y^{S_{i-1}} − (W^{S_i})^T y^{S_i} and prediction units updated by y^{S_i} ← (1−η−ϑ) y^{S_i} + ζ W^{S_i} e^{S_{i-1}} + η (W^{S_{i+1}})^T y^{S_{i+1}}, "with an appropriate choice of parameters (i.e., ζ = μ, η = ν, and ϑ = −ν) the reformulated linear predictive coding model ... can be seen to be mathematically identical to the biased competition model". Attention (top-down input) acts on the *prediction/representation* nodes, which "make the responses of the representational population more selective and also reduce the response of the error-detecting population by subtracting the top-down prediction from them". Predictions are formed at all levels ("cortical feedback connections convey predictions (outputted by a population of prediction nodes)"). Phenomena matched: Reynolds et al. 1999 and Luck et al. 1997 V2/V4 biased-competition data; fMRI reductions for coherent vs incoherent stimuli — [PMC2576514](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2576514); [CogPrints 6354](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/6354/index.html)
- Feldman & Friston (2010), *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 4:215, "Attention, uncertainty, and free-energy": attention is "inferring the level of uncertainty or precision during hierarchical perception"; "both predictions about the state of the world generating sensory data and the precision of those data have to be optimized"; "precision is encoded by the synaptic gain (post-synaptic responsiveness) of units reporting prediction errors". Precision-weighted prediction error ξ(i,u) = Π̃(i,u) ε̃(i,u); state-dependent log-precisions π(i,u) are optimised with precision = exp(π). Prediction errors are computed at every level; precision reweights them — [PMC3001758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3001758/); [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2010.00215/full)
- Feldman & Friston (2010) evidence: a Posner-paradigm simulation in which "predictive cues engage top-down predictions of increased precision that facilitate rapid processing of valid visual targets" (valid-cue RT benefit ≈ 20 ms; invalid-cue cost with wider confidence intervals; early P1 enhancement for valid and late P3 enhancement for invalid targets); simultaneous valid+invalid targets attenuated the unattended response ≈ 20%, so "biased competition emerges naturally in a Bayes-optimal fashion", matched to Luck et al. (1997) V4 data. Neuromodulatory substrates: fast gain control by gamma-band synchrony (30–100 Hz) and slow cholinergic modulation ("acetylcholine appears to increase synaptic gain directly by, for example, reducing spike-frequency adaptation") — [PMC3001758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3001758/)
- Feldman & Friston (2010) on exogenous capture: "Abrupt-onset stimuli, which have high temporal contrast, and thus salience, can attract attention even if they are task-irrelevant"; invalid targets can override the cue-induced precision bias only after accumulated evidence (time-dependent capture) — [PMC3001758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3001758/)
- Hohwy (2012), *Frontiers in Psychology* 3:96, "Attention and conscious perception in the hypothesis testing brain": "attention is simply the process of optimizing precision during hierarchical inference"; exogenous capture is reduced to precision: stimuli "with large spatial contrast and/or temporal contrast (abrupt onset) tend to 'grab' attention bottom-up" because they are assumed to have "better signal to noise ratio (better precision) than weaker signals", so error units "should thus expect high precision and be given larger gain". Inattentional blindness: under a strongly weighted task model "the invalid target may never actually be perceived". Attention does not decide *whether* error is computed but how it is weighted — [PMC3317264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3317264); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00096/full)
- Clark (2013), *BBS* 36(3):181–204, "Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science", and Clark (2013), *Frontiers in Psychology* 4:270, "The many faces of precision": precision-weighting is "altering the gain on select populations of prediction error units" (attention = "upping the gain" on error units); "very low-precision prediction errors will have little or no influence upon ongoing processing"; precision expectations are a higher-level meta-model over which strategies deploy in context — [Macquarie record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/whatever-next-predictive-brains-situated-agents-and-the-future-of/); [Frontiers 4:270](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00270/full)
- Kanai, Komura, Shipp & Friston (2015), *Phil Trans R Soc B* 370:20140169, "Cerebral hierarchies: predictive processing, precision and the pulvinar": two classes of connections — "driving (first-order) connections concerned with encoding content and modulatory (second-order) connections that establish context in the form of salience or precision". First-order predictions "enter the first-order prediction errors in a linear (subtractive) fashion. In contrast, the second-order expectations are driven by nonlinear (sum of squared) prediction errors." Precision update: "If the sum of (precision-weighted) squared prediction error is too large then expected precision falls and, conversely, rises when the sum of (precision-weighted) squared prediction error is too small." Precision is a *map* ("convergent projections from large (possibly topographically organized) regions of cortex" to the pulvinar), not a quantity for attended content only. Explicitly collapses the three constructs: "Although the concepts of salience, confidence and attention may appear distinct, their intimate relationship can be interpreted as an integral part of perceptual inference—reflecting the different faces of precision." Evidence: pulvinar neurons "sensitive to the reliability of task-relevant sensory signals, representing the 'confidence' in perceptual decisions" (Komura et al. 2013); pulvinar–V4/TEO alpha coherence enhanced when attention is directed to the receptive field (Saalmann et al. 2012) — [PMC4387510](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4387510)

**Empirical tests of the interaction (does attention scale prediction error for everything, or gate it?)**

- Kok, Rahnev, Jehee, Lau & de Lange (2012), *Cerebral Cortex* 22(9):2197–2206, "Attention reverses the effect of prediction in silencing sensory signals": fMRI, 2×2 factorial of spatial attention (which hemifield is task-relevant) × prediction (which hemifield the grating will appear in); "the attention cue had no predictive value with regard to stimulus location". Result: in the unattended field "predicted stimuli evoked a reduced response in V1 compared with unpredicted stimuli" (not significant in V2, t16 = 1.55, P = 0.139, or V3); in the attended field predicted stimuli evoked a *larger* response (V1: t16 = 2.25, P = 0.039; V2: t16 = 1.96, P = 0.067; V3: t16 = 2.67, P = 0.017) — "This reversal of prediction-related suppression by attention". Interpretation: "attention boosts the precision of predictions, leading to heightened weighting of sensory evidence (or, equivalently, prediction error), thereby reversing the effect of sensory silencing by prediction alone"; "spatial attention can be thought of as 'highlighting' a region of space, thereby increasing the precision of information coming from this region"; precision "encoded by the postsynaptic gain of neurons representing sensory data (prediction error)" (cites Treue & Martínez-Trujillo 1999; McAdams & Maunsell 2000; Reynolds & Heeger 2009) — [Rahnev lab PDF](https://rahnevlab.gatech.edu/documents/papers/Kok,Rahnev,Jehee,Lau,deLange(2012)CerebCortex.pdf); [PubMed 22047964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22047964/)
- Jiang, Summerfield & Egner (2013), *J Neurosci* 33(47):18438–18447, "Attention sharpens the distinction between expected and unexpected percepts in the visual brain": 2 (face/scene) × 2 (attended/unattended) × 2 (expected/unexpected; auditory cue gave 75% probability) design. Two competing models stated: PE-suppression (attention "dampen[s] visual PE signals", reducing the expected/unexpected disparity) vs PE-promotion (attention "enhances PEs, acting as a multiplicative scaling factor on the impact of PEs on subsequent predictions"). MVPA result: attention "greatly enhanced the distinction between expected and unexpected faces in the FFA", from "chance (50% accuracy)" to "75% accuracy" (same for scenes in PPA); the univariate ANOVA showed "no attention by expectation interaction". Conclusion: attention increases "the precision of prediction errors" — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- Auksztulewicz & Friston (2015), *Cerebral Cortex* 25(11):4273–4283, "Attentional enhancement of auditory mismatch responses: a DCM/MEG study": temporal attention (cue to attend early 600 ms vs late 1400 ms position, 100% valid) crossed with roving-oddball expectation. "Mismatch negativity (MMN) was enhanced by attention, speaking against its supposedly pre-attentive nature"; attention × expectation interaction at 193–197 ms; in the winning canonical-microcircuit DCM "attention was linked to the gain of inhibitory interneurons, consistent with its modulation of sensory precision" (as summarised: the winning model "allowed for an attentional modulation of the gain of A1 inhibitory interneurons"). As summarised from the fetched text, unattended deviants showed no significant MMN while attended deviants did — [PMC4816780](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4816780); [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1464809/)
- Smout, Tang, Garrido & Mattingley (2019), *PLoS Biology*, "Attention promotes the neural encoding of prediction errors": EEG, N = 24; gratings (9 orientations) in a roving oddball with standards, deviants and equiprobable controls; attention = monitor gratings vs monitor fixation dots. Main effect of prediction present regardless of attention ("deviant > control, 113–550 ms, cluster-corrected p < 0.001"); "attention and prediction interacted to influence the amplitude of mismatch response profiles (332–480 ms, cluster-corrected p = 0.031)"; "attention enhanced the amplitude of deviant mismatch response profiles but had little effect on those evoked by controls"; "mismatch response profiles were clearly weaker in response to controls and ignored deviants". Conclusion: "attention boosts mismatch information evoked by surprising stimuli and is consistent with a recent version of predictive coding theory that proposes attention optimises the expected precision of predictions by increasing the gain of prediction errors" — [PMC6411367](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6411367/)
- Summerfield & Egner (2009), *TiCS* 13(9):403–409, "Expectation (and attention) in visual cognition": the framing paper — attention "prioritizes stimulus processing on the basis of motivational relevance" and expectations "constrain visual interpretation on the basis of prior likelihood"; expectation "has been relatively neglected" — [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/display/pub713484)

**Critics and complications**

- Bowman, Filetti, Wyble & Olivers (2013), *BBS* 36(3):206–208, "Attention is more than prediction precision" (commentary on Clark): "A cornerstone of the target article is that, in a predictive coding framework, attention can be modelled by weighting prediction error with a measure of precision. We argue that this is not a complete explanation, especially in the light of ERP (event-related potentials) data showing large evoked responses for frequently presented target stimuli, which thus are predicted." — [Kent KAR 33892](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/33892/); [PSU record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/attention-is-more-than-prediction-precision/)
- Ransom & Fazelpour (2015), Minds Online, "Three problems for the predictive coding theory of attention": (1) feature-based endogenous attention — if the hypothesis is "this item is my keyset", non-keys generate the larger prediction errors, so "attention must be driven by high expected precision ... But this is the inverse of what is needed"; (2) non-perceptual attention — "Do we expect our thoughts to be more precise on some occasions rather than others?"; (3) affectively salient but rare events (the rarely-present aggressive dog: "you always attend to the yard when you walk by ... But notice that you don't expect a precise signal") — costs/preferences, not precision, drive attention — [Minds Online 2015](https://mindsonline.philosophyofbrains.com/2015/session4/three-problems-for-the-predictive-coding-theory-of-attention/)
- Ransom, Fazelpour & Mole (2017), *Consciousness and Cognition* 47:99–112, "Attention in the predictive mind": the precision-optimisation account "cannot accommodate voluntary shifts of attention"; Clark replied in "Predictions, precision, and agentive attention" (*Consciousness and Cognition* 2017) arguing the challenge fails — [PhilArchive listing](https://dc2.philarchive.org/s/Mole); [Clark reply, Edinburgh record](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/predictions-precision-and-agentive-attention/)
- Rungratsameetaweemana & Serences (2019), *Current Opinion in Psychology* 29, "Dissociating the impact of attention and expectation on early sensory processing": expectation operates "based on the probability of stimulus occurrence", attention "based on the behavioral relevance of different stimuli"; they are "logically distinct processes" because "the probability that a stimulus will be encountered in a given context is not necessarily linked to its behavioral relevance". When isolated, expectation "has little influence on early sensory responses and primarily influences later 'decisional' stages". Evidence: Rungratsameetaweemana et al. (2018, *J Neurosci*) — "expectations about sensory features had no impact on either of these components [early EEG markers] despite the robust effect of expectations on behavior"; Bang & Rahnev (2017) — pre- and post-cues "have similar effects"; Wyart et al. (2012) — attention "selectively lower[ed] false-alarm rates" while expectation "biased the baseline activity". Critique of Kok et al. 2012-type designs: participants "not only knew what target feature to expect, but they also knew what target feature was relevant" — [PMC6756985](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6756985)
- Alink & Blank (2021), *NeuroImage* 231:117824, "Can expectation suppression be explained by reduced attention to predictable stimuli?": proposes that expectation suppression "could be explained by a reduced attention effect, specifically through reduced saliency-driven allocation of attention" rather than reduced prediction error; resolving it "requires assessing qualitative differences between how attention and surprise enhance brain responses" — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/7e7b0dbdac9045e7b23cd01829c8dce7); [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.06.19.154427.full.pdf)
- Press, Kok & Yon (2020), *TiCS* 24(1):13–24, "The perceptual prediction paradox": Bayesian models hold "predispositions to perceive what we expect will generate more veridical experiences" while cancellation models hold we should "perceptually prioritise the unexpected because it is informative". Resolution: "We may be initially predisposed to perceive what we expect to generate broadly accurate perceptual representations. However, later processes subsequently highlight unexpected signals that are sufficiently 'surprising'"; "We will therefore broadly perceive what we expect, unless unexpected signals are likely to be informative for model updating." — [Goldsmiths record + PDF](https://research.gold.ac.uk/28217)
- Slagter & van Moorselaar (2021), *Visual Cognition* 29(9):631–636, "Attention and distraction in the predictive brain": attentional capture "is a logical consequence of the overall imperative of the brain to predict what sensory signals provide precise information to achieve goal-directed behaviour"; high-contrast stimuli receive "high precision" by default (signal-to-noise), while attention is flexible precision re-weighting by task and learning; the three capture sources (physical salience, implicit learning, explicit goals) all run through precision — [PMC8547734](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8547734)
- "Marvan & Havlík" on attention-as-precision and capture: UNVERIFIED as attributed. Tomáš Marvan and Marek Havlík exist and co-authored "Is Predictive Processing a Theory of Consciousness?" (*New Ideas in Psychology*, 2021; PhilSci preprint 2020), which concerns consciousness, not attentional capture. No paper by them on precision and capture by the unexpected was found — [PhilSci Archive 18426](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/18426)

### Inferences
- Across the predictive-coding family, "attention in-region" is not a modelling option: every formulation computes ε everywhere and lets Π do the selecting. In a system that forms expectations only inside admitted regions, the Π-outside-the-region term would be identically zero, which is the degenerate limit the theories explicitly avoid (Hohwy: attention does not decide *whether* error is computed).
- The empirical pattern most consistent across Kok 2012, Jiang 2013, Smout 2019 is multiplicative: response ∝ gain(attention) × |prediction error|, with gain > 0 outside the attended set (Smout's ignored deviants still show mismatch; Kok's unattended-predicted stimuli still show suppression, which requires a prediction to have been made there). Auksztulewicz & Friston 2015 is the outlier where the unattended MMN did not reach significance.
- The critics divide into two kinds that matter for design: (a) precision cannot be the whole story because capture by rare/irrelevant/affective events and voluntary feature-search point to a term that is neither relevance-gain nor the first-order prior (Bowman; Ransom & Fazelpour; Ransom et al.); (b) much of what is called "expectation suppression" may be attention/salience in disguise (Alink & Blank; Rungratsameetaweemana & Serences), so expectation and attention must be manipulated orthogonally before either is credited.
- Press, Kok & Yon's two-stage rule (perceive what you expect, then promote the unexpected only if informative) is the closest published statement of a surprise-with-threshold gate that is separate from both the prior and the relevance gain.

## KQ2. Attention vs salience in active inference and Bayesian theories: is novelty / expected information gain a separate term from relevance (attention) and from the prior (expectation), and how is it formalised?

### Takeaway
Active inference makes a three-way formal split: **attention** = precision (gain) on sensory prediction errors; **salience** = epistemic value = expected information gain about hidden *states* (a property of policies/saccades, not stimuli); **novelty** = expected information gain about model *parameters* (Friston et al. 2015; Mirza et al. 2019; Schwartenbeck et al. 2019; Parr & Friston 2019). Itti & Baldi (2009) formalise surprise as KL(posterior ‖ prior) computed at every location, and Yu & Dayan (2005) split uncertainty into expected (ACh, cue unreliability) vs unexpected (NE, context change). Chikkerur et al. (2010) make attention itself Bayesian inference over "what" and "where" with task priors, with no separate novelty term. Kanai et al. (2015) is the dissenting voice that folds salience back into "faces of precision".

### Cited Findings

- Parr & Friston (2019), *Current Opinion in Psychology* 29:1–5, "Attention or salience?": "a distinction between attentional gain mechanisms and salience attribution, where the former depends upon estimating the precision of sensory data, while the latter is a consequence of the need to actively engage with the sensorium"; "salience is something that is afforded to actions that realise epistemic affordance, while attention per se is afforded to precise sensory evidence - or beliefs about the causes of sensations"; "there is a fundamental distinction between making some attributes more salient – by biasing their contribution to epistemic affordance – and simply adjusting the attentional gain (i.e., precision) of different sensory streams. Selective attention becomes part of planning a saccadic eye movement, not simply attending to the sensory consequences of that movement." (quotes as returned from the UCL record/abstract) — [UCL Discovery 10058428](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10058428/)
- Friston, Rigoli, Ognibene, Mathys, Fitzgerald & Pezzulo (2015), *Cognitive Neuroscience* 6(4):187–214, "Active inference and epistemic value": agents minimise expected free energy G(π,τ); "The negative free energy or quality of a policy can be decomposed into extrinsic and epistemic (or intrinsic) value"; "Epistemic value is simply the expected information gain (mutual information) afforded to hidden states by future outcomes"; alternative decomposition G(π) = D_KL[Q(o|π) ‖ P(o)] − H[o|s,π] ("risk and ambiguity"); "Maximising epistemic value is equivalent to maximising (expected) Bayesian surprise"; "Epistemic value can be regarded as the drive for novelty seeking behaviour"; policy selection P(π) ∝ exp(−γ G(π)) with precision γ (dopamine); T-maze simulation: trials 1–20 visit the cue (epistemic phase), then direct reward-seeking — "epistemic (ambiguity-resolving) behaviour enables pragmatic (reward-seeking) behaviour". The paper does **not** separate state-salience from parameter-novelty — [PMC5167251](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5167251); [UEA record](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/active-inference-and-epistemic-value/)
- Mirza, Adams, Friston & Parr (2019), *Scientific Reports* (doi 10.1038/s41598-019-50138-8), "Introducing a Bayesian model of selective attention based on active inference": "attention corresponds to optimisation of the precision (i.e., confidence) afforded sensory evidence", implemented as context-dependent precision ζ on the likelihood, P(o_m = n | s) = σ(ζ_im ln Ā_nijklm) (Eq. 6); expected free energy "comprises three terms: novelty, epistemic and extrinsic value" (Eq. 4), epistemic term −E_Q[ln Q(s_τ|o_τ,π) − ln Q(s_τ|π)]; "A policy has epistemic value (i.e., has epistemic affordance), if the outcomes expected under that policy resolve uncertainty about hidden states – this is sometimes referred to as salience"; "Novelty is the information that can be gained about the parameters of the generative model" — a policy carries novelty value when "it affords the opportunity to reduce uncertainty about contingencies (encoded in the likelihood matrix, transition matrix and initial state probabilities)". Simulations: Yarbus-style task-dependent exploration; autism as "failure to down-modulate the precision of task-irrelevant sensory signals" — [PMC6763492](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6763492)
- Schwartenbeck, Passecker, Hauser, FitzGerald, Kronbichler & Friston (2019), *eLife* 8:e41703, "Computational mechanisms of curiosity and goal-directed exploration": two separable drives — "'Hidden state' exploration motivates agents to sample unambiguous observations to accurately infer the (hidden) state of the world" (active inference) vs "'Model parameter' exploration compels agents to sample outcomes associated with high uncertainty, if they are informative for their representation of the task structure" (active learning); the slogan "'salience is to inference' as 'novelty is to learning'"; novelty is computed from Dirichlet concentration parameters over the likelihood mapping; the two "can make opposing predictions about behaviour". Contrast with RL: UCB "adds an uncertainty bonus to options that have not been sampled for a long time", whereas here the bonus is "expected information gain about model parameters" rather than visit counts — [PMC6510535](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6510535); [eLife PDF](https://elifesciences.org/articles/41703.pdf)
- Itti & Baldi (2009), *Vision Research* 49(10):1295–1306, "Bayesian surprise attracts human attention": surprise S(D,ℳ) = KL(P(M), P(M|D)) = ∫ P(M) log P(M)/P(M|D) dM, measured in "wows" ("the amount of surprise corresponding to a two-fold variation between the prior and the posterior"); "Surprise is computed for every pixel in each of the 72 center-surround feature maps" (9 pyramid levels, 5 temporal scales, 432,000 detectors; Poisson data with Gamma conjugate prior; combined S = [S_T + S_S/20]^{1/3}). Evidence: 8 + 8 observers, 50 clips, 10,192 + 6,648 saccades; "72% of all gaze shifts directed towards locations more surprising than the average, a figure rising to 84%" for inter-observer-agreed regions; AUC ≈ 0.70 vs inter-observer ceiling 0.805 ± 0.002 / 0.827 ± 0.002, variance baseline ≈ 0.6, chance 0.5; Michelson contrast "performs slightly below chance level"; surprise beats variance (t-tests, p < 10⁻¹⁰). Surprise ≠ Shannon information: "Many Bits with Few Wows" (rare but belief-preserving data) and "Few Bits with Many Wows" — [PMC2860069](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2860069); [iLab page](https://ilab.usc.edu/publications/Itti_Baldi09vr.html)
- Yu & Dayan (2005), *Neuron* 46(4):681–692, "Uncertainty, neuromodulation, and attention": "acetylcholine signals expected uncertainty, coming from known unreliability of predictive cues within a context, while norepinephrine signals unexpected uncertainty, as when unsignaled context switches produce strongly unexpected observations"; the model "suggests a class of attentional cueing tasks that involve both neuromodulators" whose interactions are "part-antagonistic, part-synergistic". Precursor: Yu & Dayan NIPS 2002, "Expected and Unexpected Uncertainty: ACh and NE in the Neocortex" — NE signals "the radical divergence of bottom-up inputs from prevailing top-down interpretations" — [NTU record](https://scholars.lib.ntu.edu.tw/handle/123456789/625612); [NIPS 2002 abstract](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2002/hash/758a06618c69880a6cee5314ee42d52f-Abstract.html)
- Chikkerur, Serre, Tan & Poggio (2010), *Vision Research* 50(22):2233–2247, "What and where: a Bayesian inference theory of attention": "Attention is part of the inference process that solves the visual recognition problem of what is where"; "spatial attention emerges as a strategy to reduce the uncertainty in shape information while feature-based attention reduces the uncertainty in spatial information"; predicted phenomena: bottom-up pop-out, "multiplicative modulation of neuronal tuning curves", "shift in contrast responses", human fixations in natural scenes; improves recognition in clutter. No novelty/surprise term in the abstract — [MIT DSpace 1721.1/62387](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/62387)
- Kanai et al. (2015) — the opposing "one quantity" position: salience, confidence and attention are "the different faces of precision" — [PMC4387510](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4387510)
- Slagter & van Moorselaar (2021) — physical salience is default high precision; the paper "does not explicitly name 'expected information gain' or 'epistemic value'" (as summarised) — [PMC8547734](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8547734)
- Classical neurophysiology already treats novelty as a *separate* bias (cross-reference to KQ3): Desimone & Duncan (1995) list "bias to novelty" under "bias derived from long-term memory", separate from the top-down attentional template and from stimulus salience; "both novel stimuli and stimuli that have not been recently seen will have a larger neural signal in the visual cortex, giving them a competitive advantage" (pp. 201–202) — [Cornell PDF](https://confluence.cornell.edu/download/attachments/89461995/Desimone%20and%20Duncan%20-%201995%20-%20Neural%20Mechanisms%20of%20Selective%20Visual%20Attention.pdf)

### Inferences
- The field's most explicit formal answer to "should novelty be a separate steering term?" is yes, twice over: the active-inference EFE has *three* additive terms (extrinsic value, state-epistemic "salience", parameter-epistemic "novelty"), each distinct from precision (attention) and from the first-order prior (expectation). Precision sits on the likelihood/prediction errors; salience and novelty sit on policies (where to look next).
- Itti & Baldi's surprise is a *map* over the whole field, i.e., it presupposes a prior and posterior at every location — surprise cannot be computed where no expectation was formed. This is the same "prediction everywhere" commitment as predictive coding, now for the steering signal.
- Yu & Dayan's split suggests two different uses of surprise: expected uncertainty (how much to trust the top-down cue vs bottom-up data, a precision-like gain) vs unexpected uncertainty (a reset/interrupt signal that reallocates). The latter is the mechanism closest to "capture by the unexpected" that the precision critics say is missing.
- Schwartenbeck's "salience is to inference as novelty is to learning" gives a usable criterion: salience resolves uncertainty about *which state* is present now; novelty resolves uncertainty about *the model* (weights). A system that learns its mask could in principle attach a novelty bonus to the mask's parameters' uncertainty rather than to prediction error magnitude.

- Yu & Dayan's exact equations (expected uncertainty = 1 − cue validity; unexpected uncertainty from the posterior over context change) were not retrievable from an open page; only the abstract-level definitions are cited.
- Chikkerur et al.'s graphical-model factorization (O, L, F_i, X_i, I) was not extracted (PDF redirect); only the abstract claims are cited.

## KQ3. Classical theories: filter, attenuation, feature integration, biased competition, normalization, TVA, Guided Search — which put prediction inside or outside attention?

### Takeaway
The classical theories split cleanly. **Prediction outside attention**: Treisman's attenuation (context lowers recognition thresholds on the *unattended* channel), FIT ("top-down processing of unattended features is capable of utilizing past experience and contextual information"), TVA (pigeonholing bias β_i is a per-category prior/utility term separate from the per-object attentional weight w_x), biased competition (the "attentional template" is a working-memory description; novelty/familiarity is an independent LTM-derived bias), and Guided Search 6.0 (history/priming and scene priors are separate sources summed into the priority map). **No prediction term at all**: Broadbent's all-or-none filter and Reynolds & Heeger's normalization model (attention field = pure relevance gain, 1 everywhere else). None of them puts prediction *inside* attention in the sense of predicting only what is attended; FIT in fact asserts that unattended features are still conjoined by context, just unreliably.

### Cited Findings

- Broadbent (1958), *Perception and Communication* — the early-selection filter: selection by physical characteristics before semantic analysis; Treisman (1960) showed this is too strong. In Treisman's dichotic-shadowing study ("Contextual cues in selective listening", *QJEP* 12:242–248), when passages switched ears "subjects occasionally repeated one or two words from the wrong ear at the break"; "The higher the transition probabilities in the passage, the more likely subjects were to do this"; Treisman's explanation: the "selective filter" (Broadbent, 1958) acts "by selectively raising thresholds for signals from rejected sources rather than acting as an all-or-none barrier" (summary of the primary; the primary was not fetched) — [Wikipedia: Attenuation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attenuation_theory); [OpenLearn summary](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/society-politics-law/sociology/attention/content-section-1.4)
- Treisman & Gelade (1980), *Cognitive Psychology* 12:97–136, "A feature-integration theory of attention": "features are registered early, automatically, and in parallel across the visual field, while objects are identified separately and only at a later stage, which requires focused attention"; "stimulus locations are processed serially with focal attention. Any features which are present in the same central 'fixation' of attention are combined to form a single object. Thus focal attention provides the 'glue'"; "if focused attention is prevented, the features of unattended objects may be free floating spatially, as well as unrelated to one another". Crucially on expectation outside attention: "Our claim is that attention is necessary for the correct perception of conjunctions, although unattended features are also conjoined prior to conscious perception. The top-down processing of unattended features is capable of utilizing past experience and contextual information. Even when attention is directed elsewhere, we are unlikely to see a blue sun in a yellow sky. However, in the absence of focused attention and of effective constraints on top-down processing, conjunctions of features could be formed on a random basis. These unattended couplings will give rise to 'illusory conjunctions.'" Conjunction search is "serial and self-terminating" (positive slopes ≈ half of negatives) — [Princeton course PDF](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spring08/cos598B/Readings/TreismanGelade1980.pdf)
- Treisman & Schmidt (1982), *Cognitive Psychology* 14(1):107–141, "Illusory conjunctions in the perception of objects": brief displays masked by random dots with attention diverted to flanking digits; participants reported shapes "made up of a combination of features from two different stimuli" (secondary source gives ≈18% of trials) — [Wikipedia: Feature integration theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_integration_theory); critical follow-up: Prinzmetal and colleagues on whether illusory conjunctions require inattention — [Ivry lab PDF, Ivry & Prinzmetal 1991](https://ivrylab.berkeley.edu/files/organized_pubs_pdfs/1991_ivry_r_and_prinzmetal_w_e.pdf)
- Desimone & Duncan (1995), *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 18:193–222, "Neural mechanisms of selective visual attention": "objects in the visual input compete for representation, analysis, or control. The competition is biased, however, towards information that is currently relevant to behavior." Against the "mental spotlight": "the model we develop is that attention is an emergent property of many neural mechanisms working to resolve competition for visual processing and control of behavior." Top-down bias: "Some kind of short-term description of the information currently needed must be used to control competitive bias in the visual system, such that inputs matching that description are favored in the visual cortex (Bundesen 1990, Duncan & Humphreys 1989). Novelty as a *third* bias: "A final consideration is bias derived from long-term memory. One interesting case is bias to novelty ... it is much easier to find an inverted (novel) target among upright (familiar) targets (Figure 4c) than the reverse" (Reicher et al. 1976; Wang et al. 1992), implying "familiarity is a type of object feature that can be used to bias attentional competition"; IT repetition suppression: "For up to a third of the cells in this region, responses to novel sample stimuli become suppressed as the animal acquires familiarity" and suppression for recently seen stimuli is "an automatic outcome of any stimulus repetition (Miller & Desimone 1994)", so "both novel stimuli and stimuli that have not been recently seen will have a larger neural signal in the visual cortex, giving them a competitive advantage" (pp. 201–202). Also: "the top-down selection bias of a current task can sometimes be overturned by information of long-term or general significance acting in a bottom-up fashion" (own name, Moray 1959) — [Cornell PDF](https://confluence.cornell.edu/download/attachments/89461995/Desimone%20and%20Duncan%20-%201995%20-%20Neural%20Mechanisms%20of%20Selective%20Visual%20Attention.pdf); [MRC CBU record](https://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/bibliography/articles/3148)
- Reynolds & Heeger (2009), *Neuron* 61(2):168–185, "The normalization model of attention": R(x,θ) = ⌊A(x,θ)E(x,θ) / (S(x,θ) + σ)⌋_T with stimulus drive E, attention field A, suppressive drive S = s(x,θ) ∗ [A E]; "The attention field is multiplied by the stimulus drive before normaliz[ation]"; "The attention field is 1 everywhere except for a small range of spatial positions and feature values where the attentional gain is greater than 1." The same model yields contrast gain (small stimulus, large attention field: Reynolds et al. 2000) or response gain (large stimulus, small attention field), and reconciles Martinez-Trujillo & Treue 2002 and Williford & Maunsell 2006; it unifies biased competition, feature-similarity gain (Treue & Martinez-Trujillo 1999) and contrast-gain control (Reynolds, Chelazzi & Desimone 1999): "the various models outlined above are not mutually exclusive. No expectation/prediction term; A encodes task relevance only — [PMC2752446](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2752446); [Heeger lab PDF](https://www.cns.nyu.edu/heegerlab/content/publications/Reynolds-Neuron2009.pdf)
- Bundesen (1990), *Psychological Review* 97(4):523–547, "A theory of visual attention" (TVA): all categorizations "x belongs to i" race to be encoded in VSTM (capacity K); rate equation v(x,i) = η(x,i) β_i w_x / Σ_z w_z where η(x,i) is "the strength of the sensory evidence that object x has feature i" and β_i "the perceptual bias in favor of categorizing object x as feature i" (pigeonholing); weight equation w_x = Σ_j η(x,j) π_j where π_j (pertinence) "is a measure of the momentary importance of attending to objects that belong to category j" (filtering); "the attentional weight of an object is a sum of the η*π products for all features". "Each of the possible categorizations is supported by sensory evidence, but the competition is biased by multiplication with attentional weights (high weights on important objects) and perceptual biases (toward use of important categories)." In NTVA (Bundesen, Habekost & Kyllingsbæk 2005) filtering = "dynamic remapping of receptive fields" and pigeonholing = "multiplicative scaling of the level of activation in cells coding for particular features" — [Springer PB&R TVA article](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-017-1337-1); [PhilArchive BUNTOV](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/BUNTOV); [Poth et al. 2014, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00930/full)
- Bundesen, Vangkilde & Habekost, "Components of visual bias: a multiplicative hypothesis": visual bias toward seeing object x as a member of category i is "a product of three factors: the expectancy (prior probability) of being presented with members of category i, the subjective importance (utility) of seeing objects in category i as members of that category, and the general level of alertness" (search-engine summary of the KU record; the record page itself returned 404 on fetch — venue/year UNVERIFIED here) — [KU research record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/components-of-visual-bias-a-multiplicative-hypothesis)
- Wolfe (2021), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 28:1060–1092, "Guided Search 6.0: An updated model of visual search": "guidance comes from five sources of preattentive information: (1) top-down and (2) bottom-up feature guidance, (3) prior history (e.g., priming), (4) reward, and (5) scene syntax and semantics. These sources are combined into a spatial 'priority map'"; "a priority map based on a weighted average of all the various forms of guidance"; "Selective attention is guided to the most active location in the priority map approximately 20 times per second"; "binding and recognition of an attended object is modeled as a diffusion process taking > 150 ms/item". History vs goals: "'history' effects ... arise from passive exposure to some sequence of stimuli (e.g., priming effects)", e.g., Maljkovic & Nakayama's priming of pop-out; these "represent ways to change the landscape of the priority map that are not based on salience or the observer's goals" (cites Awh et al. 2012's "selection history"). Templates: a "guiding template" in WM and a "target template" in activated LTM; the guiding template "can also be shaped by the prior history of search ... These priming effects on the template will occur implicitly". Bottom-up salience is "a very powerful but short-lived form of guidance (Donk & van Zoest, 2008)"; capture = "bumps in the priority map" (Lamy et al. 2020) — [Harvard/BWH PDF](https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/Wolfe2021_GS6.pdf)

### Inferences
- The classical consensus treats the prior as acting on the *unattended* field: Treisman's attenuated channel is analysed against lowered thresholds, FIT lets context "glue" unattended features (badly), TVA's β_i applies to every object's categorizations regardless of its w_x, and GS6's priority map sums history/scene priors with salience before selection. Only the gain models (Broadbent's filter, Reynolds & Heeger) have no prior at all, and none restricts prediction to the attended region.
- TVA is the cleanest formal statement of the relevance/prior split the design question asks about: w_x (filtering, where to look, normalised across objects) and β_i (pigeonholing, which category to expect/value) are separate multiplicands of a shared sensory evidence term η — exactly a "mask × prior × evidence" factorization. The multiplicative hypothesis then factors β_i itself into prior probability × utility × alertness.
- Biased competition and GS6 both locate novelty/history as a *third* independent bias (LTM-derived, "not based on salience or the observer's goals"), consistent with the active-inference split in KQ2; in BC the mechanism is repetition suppression (familiar items produce smaller signals), i.e., novelty is implemented as the complement of a learned expectation rather than as a separately computed quantity.
- The GS6 timing (selection ≈ 20 Hz, recognition > 150 ms/item, asynchronous diffusers) is a serial-select/parallel-verify hybrid that is architecturally close to a serial reader with a short-term buffer.

## KQ4. ML hard attention and credit assignment: what do hard-attention models do about unattended items receiving no gradient (collapse), and what remedies exist?

### Takeaway
Hard attention (one-hot selection) makes the objective non-differentiable with respect to the selector and gives unselected items zero gradient; the standard fix is to treat the selection as a stochastic policy and use REINFORCE/variational bounds with variance reduction (baselines, entropy bonuses, mixing in the expected/soft value), or to relax the discrete choice (Gumbel-Softmax/Concrete, straight-through, REBAR/RELAX). The collapse failure is documented most explicitly in sparse mixture-of-experts routing ("self-reinforcing imbalance") and remedied by injected noise, k > 1 selection, auxiliary load/importance balancing losses, capacity limits, and pretraining/warm-starting the selector (Saccader; Bengio et al. 2015's per-batch and per-example usage regularisers).

### Cited Findings

- Mnih, Heess, Graves & Kavukcuoglu (2014), NIPS, "Recurrent models of visual attention": a glimpse sensor (k square patches around location l, each twice the width of the previous), core RNN h_t = f_h(h_{t−1}, g_t; θ_h), location network f_l (Gaussian policy, "a two-component Gaussian with a fixed variance"), action network f_a. "While the model is non-differentiable, it can be trained using reinforcement learning methods"; the location network "is always trained with REINFORCE" with gradient ∇_θ J ≈ (1/M) Σ Σ ∇_θ log π(u_t | s_{1:t}; θ)(R_t − b_t), b_t = E_π[R_t]; framed as "a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)" — the model sees only what it attends to and learns where to look from reward alone. ### Inferences
- The collapse mechanism in MoE routing is isomorphic to the design concern: items (experts) that are not selected get no gradient, the selected ones improve, and the selector's preference for them hardens — Shazeer's "self-reinforcing imbalance". Every remedy in the literature adds *one* of: (i) exploration noise in the selector (noisy top-k; fixed-variance Gaussian in RAM; entropy bonus in Xu), (ii) leakage of gradient to non-selected items (k > 1; soft/hard mixing with p = 0.5; straight-through; relaxations), (iii) an explicit usage prior enforced by an auxiliary loss (importance/load/balance losses; Bengio's L_b, L_e, L_v; Xu's doubly-stochastic penalty), or (iv) a warm start that places the selector in a sensible basin before hard selection (Saccader pretraining; Eigen's early balance constraint).
- Xu's doubly-stochastic regulariser and Bengio's L_b are the closest analogues to an "attend-all-eventually" prior: they penalise regions that are never admitted over a horizon without forcing uniform admission at any single step.
- REINFORCE-style training of the mask (reward = downstream likelihood) is the only route that trains the selector on what it did *not* admit — all pathwise methods (soft weights trained through admitted items) are silent about the counterfactual. Variational hard-attention (Lawson; Deng) and control-variate estimators (REBAR/RELAX) exist precisely because plain REINFORCE on the selector is too high-variance.

### Gaps
- No single paper was found that analyses "attention collapse" in hard visual attention as a named phenomenon with a measured frequency; the collapse literature is concentrated in MoE routing.
- Full text of Saccader's pretraining objective was not fetched; only the abstract is cited.
- "Attend-all warm start" as a named technique was not found in the literature; the nearest documented practices are Saccader's pretraining, Xu's 0.5 soft/hard mixing, and the balance constraints.

## KQ5. Novelty / curiosity as intrinsic salience in ML: how is novelty defined (prediction error vs count vs information gain) and what are the known pathologies?

### Takeaway
Three formal families define novelty: (i) prediction error (Schmidhuber 1991; Pathak et al. 2017 ICM; RND), (ii) visit counts generalised to pseudo-counts from a density model (Bellemare et al. 2016), and (iii) information gain / learning progress (Schmidhuber 2010 compression progress; Oudeyer & Kaplan 2007 LPM; Bayesian surprise). Bellemare et al. prove IG ≤ PG ≤ 1/N̂, tying the three together. The canonical pathology is the noisy-TV problem: raw prediction-error bonuses are attracted to irreducible stochasticity; remedies are to predict only agent-controllable features (ICM), to predict a deterministic target inside the predictor's model class (RND), to reward learning *progress* rather than error (Schmidhuber; Oudeyer & Kaplan), or to separate aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty.

### Cited Findings

- Schmidhuber (1991), "Curious model-building control systems", *Proc. IJCNN Singapore* 2:1458–1463: a system that "actively tries to provoke situations for which it learned to expect to learn something about the environment", maximising "the expectation of the temporal derivative of the adaptive assumed reliability of future predictions"; curiosity defined as "the desire to improve a predictor of the reactions of an environment (a 'world model')" — [ML Anthology record](https://mlanthology.org/misc/1991/schmidhuber1991misc-curious); [TUM mediaTUM](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/node?id=814953)
- Schmidhuber (2010), *IEEE Trans. The learning progress of (1) is the fun or intrinsic reward of (2)" — [UVic PDF](https://www.ece.uvic.ca/~bctill/papers/ememcog/Schmidhuber_2010.pdf)
- Oudeyer & Kaplan (2007), *Frontiers in Neurorobotics* 1:6, "What is intrinsic motivation? ### Inferences
- The ML literature converges with Oudeyer & Kaplan's taxonomy: raw prediction error as novelty is the simplest and the most pathological; count/pseudo-count and information-gain bonuses are better behaved because they fall with exposure *regardless of residual error*; learning progress (error decrease) is the classical answer to irreducible noise.
- RND's decomposition is the practical checklist for a surprise signal: a surprise term that is to steer attention should be driven by factor 1 (little data) and not by factors 2–4 (stochastic target, missing information, optimizer failure). A prediction made per region against arriving content will carry all four unless the predicted quantity is deterministic given the model (RND's trick) or restricted to what the system itself can influence (ICM's trick).
- Bellemare's inequality IG ≤ PG ≤ 1/N̂ gives a formal licence to treat "how much the prior moved" (Bayesian surprise / Itti–Baldi wows) and "how rarely seen" (counts) as the same quantity up to bounds — relevant if a system wants one novelty scalar per concept.

### Gaps
- The Mavor-Parker 2021 and Hou 2025 papers were identified by title only; their mechanisms are not summarised here.
- No source was found that benchmarks prediction-error vs count vs information-gain novelty bonuses specifically for *attention steering* (as opposed to RL exploration); the transfer to a reading architecture is an inference.
