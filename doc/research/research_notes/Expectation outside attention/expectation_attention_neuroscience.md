# Expectation vs attention in sensory neuroscience and psychophysics: is prediction (and prediction error) computed outside attention?

Scope note. Vision is the focus; audition appears only as contrast. Every citation below was checked against a search result, a Crossref record, Europe PMC or the article page in this session (date 2026-10-09). Where I could reach only an abstract, that is marked "abstract-level". Anything not verified is marked UNVERIFIED. Statistics quoted are copied from the papers' full text as retrieved (PMC/eLife/PDF), not from memory. Terminology used throughout: "expectation suppression" (ES) = smaller response to an expected than to an unexpected (or neutral) stimulus; "attended" means task-relevant and/or inside the spatial focus; "unattended/ignored" means task-irrelevant AND attention drawn elsewhere by a competing task, unless noted.

---

## Q1. Does expectation suppression occur for unattended/ignored stimuli, or only attended ones?

### Takeaway
The two best-powered fMRI studies that actively drew attention away with a competing task (faces: Larsson & Smith 2012, N=8; objects: Richter & de Lange 2019, N=34, preregistered) found that expectation effects vanished for unattended stimuli while stimulus processing (repetition suppression; decodable object identity) persisted. But three other visual fMRI studies (Kok et al. 2012 Cereb Cortex; St. John-Saaltink et al. 2015; Ekman et al. 2017) found expectation effects on task-irrelevant, spatially unattended gratings/dots, so the evidence does not support the strong claim that prediction is never computed outside attention; it supports a narrower claim that the "suppression-for-expected" signature for complex, learned object/face associations needs attention, while simple cued or sequence predictions in V1 can survive diverted attention.

### Cited Findings

**Larsson & Smith 2012 (primary, fMRI, faces)**
- Citation: Larsson, J., & Smith, A. T. (2012). fMRI repetition suppression: neuronal adaptation or stimulus expectation? *Cerebral Cortex*, 22(3), 567–576 (advance access 2011). — [PMC3278317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278317)
- Design: 8 subjects (all female, 20–38 y), grayscale faces 14°×14°, Summerfield-type repetition-probability manipulation (FREQ runs: 27 SAME / 9 DIFF trials, i.e. 3:1; INFREQ runs reversed), 4-s adapter, 0.5-s blank, 1-s probe. Two attention conditions: FOCUS (covertly monitor faces for rare inverted faces) and DIVERT (demanding RSVP letter task at fixation, count "X" among distractors, running asynchronously with the faces). — [PMC3278317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278317)
- Attended result: repetition suppression was significantly larger in FREQ than INFREQ runs; "perceptual expectation indexes" > 0 (P < 0.01) in all areas except VO1 (areas: V1, V2, V3, hV4, LO1, LO2, V3A/B, V7, MT, VO1, FFA), mean index ≈ 0.5, i.e. the expectation effect was about as big as the adaptation effect. FFA: robust RS in FREQ (t7 = 4.65, P < 0.01), significantly reduced in INFREQ (P < 0.05). Effect generalised to 4-s (long) adaptation. — [PMC3278317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278317)
- Unattended result: "no significant differences in the magnitude of repetition suppression between ... FREQ scans and ... INFREQ scans (paired t-test, P > 0.05 in all areas)"; expectation indexes not > 0 in any area (P > 0.2); yet "significant response suppression (reduced responses to repeated stimuli) was still observed". RSVP performance was above chance (mean 0.60, range 0.41–0.87). — [PMC3278317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278317)
- Interpretation offered by the authors: an attentional explanation (subjects attend more strongly to infrequent trials, boosting responses regardless of repetition) "seems more parsimonious" than prediction error, citing "the lack of corresponding data on prediction-error signals at the individual neuron level"; they conclude "perceptual expectation alone cannot explain fMRI adaptation". Limitations acknowledged: N = 8, 3:1 ratio may be unusually high, cannot separate spatial from feature-based attention, loss of sensitivity under inattention. — [PMC3278317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278317)

**Richter & de Lange 2019 (primary, fMRI, objects, preregistered)**
- Citation: Richter, D., & de Lange, F. P. (2019). Statistical learning attenuates visual activity only for attended stimuli. *eLife*, 8, e47869. doi 10.7554/eLife.47869 — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/47869); full text [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/)
- Design: 34 analysed (38 recruited; preregistered exclusions). Day 1: 960 learning trials, 12 leading → 12 trailing object images, P(trailing | leading) = 1.0, oddball (upside-down) detection task. Day 2 (fMRI): contingencies P(expected | leading) = 0.5 vs 0.1 per unexpected image (likelihood ratio 5:1). Objects peripheral (~5°×5°). Attended condition = categorise trailing object (electronic / non-electronic). Unattended condition = identical displays, but categorise letters vs symbols appearing inside the 0.6° fixation bull's-eye. Both tasks at ceiling (94.3 ± 5.4 % vs 94.0 ± 6.6 % correct; RT 574 ± 150 vs 602 ± 131 ms). — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/)
- Attended ES (expected < unexpected): V1 t(33) = 3.573, p = 0.001, dz = 0.613; LOC t(33) = 3.860, p = 5.0e-4, dz = 0.662; TOFC t(33) = 5.133, p = 1.2e-5, dz = 0.880. — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/)
- Unattended ES: V1 t(33) = −0.216, p = 0.830, dz = −0.037, BF10 = 0.188; LOC t(33) = −0.831, p = 0.412, dz = −0.143, BF10 = 0.253; TOFC t(33) = 0.072, p = 0.943, dz = 0.012, BF10 = 0.184 ("moderate support for the null"). Expectation × attention interaction: V1 F(1,33) = 7.706, p = 0.009, η² = 0.189; LOC F = 12.580, p = 0.001, η² = 0.276; TOFC F = 16.955, p = 2.4e-4, η² = 0.339. Whole-brain: extensive ventral-stream ES clusters when attended, none when unattended. — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/)
- The unattended objects were still processed: object identity decoded above chance (16.7 %) in V1 84.8 %, LOC 38.0 %, TOFC 25.0 % (all p ≤ 0.002), with clearly visible BOLD responses in all ROIs. — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/)
- The regularities were learned for both sets: post-scan pair recognition 62.1 ± 1.8 % (attended) vs 58.7 ± 2.2 % (unattended), both > chance, no difference (W = 365, p = 0.256, BF10 = 0.737). Behavioural benefit of expectation only when objects were the task (RT W = 9, p = 3.8e-9; none in the character task, BF10 = 0.273; RT interaction F(1,33) = 37.543, p = 6.6e-7). — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/)
- Authors' synthesis: results "corroborate and extend" Larsson & Smith 2012 but "appear at odds with" den Ouden et al. 2009 and Kok et al. 2012a,b, which found ES for task-irrelevant stimuli; the crucial difference is that there "attention was not effectively drawn away by a competing stimulus that required attention", since "any supraliminal stimulus, in the absence of competition, will be attended to some degree". Conclusion: ES "occurs irrespective of exact task goals and relevance of the predictable objects ... but it is abolished by drawing attention away from the stimuli", i.e. "the integration of prior knowledge and sensory input is gated by attention". Two mechanisms offered: (i) attention sets the gain of prediction-error units (Feldman & Friston 2010), (ii) attention is (a modulation of) the prior itself (Rao 2005; Yu & Dayan 2005), so that without attention "object predictions are not generated". Caveat they state: more "stubborn" priors learned over longer timescales, or predictions for simple stimuli needing less recurrent processing, "may persist even when attention is drawn away". — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/)
- The character task was "of trivial difficulty", so the authors argue attention was not exhaustively consumed; the manipulation is spatial/task-based (objects at ~5°, characters at fixation). — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/)

**Contrary / complicating primary studies (expectation effects for task-irrelevant or spatially unattended stimuli)**
- Kok, Rahnev, Jehee, Lau & de Lange 2012 (*Cereb Cortex* 22(9), 2197–2206): gratings in the UNattended hemifield showed reduced V1 response when spatially predicted (75 %) vs unpredicted (25 %): t16 = 2.40, P = 0.029 (not significant in V2 t16 = 1.55, V3 t16 = 1.01). Here "unattended" = other hemifield, no competing stimulus at the moment of presentation. — [Kok et al. 2012 PDF](https://rahnevlab.gatech.edu/documents/papers/Kok,Rahnev,Jehee,Lau,deLange(2012)CerebCortex.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhr310)
- St. John-Saaltink, Utzerath, Kok, Lau & de Lange 2015, "Expectation suppression in early visual cortex depends on task set", *PLoS ONE* 10: e0131172 (N = 33; 2 excluded): peripheral grating annulus (15° outer diameter), auditory tone predicting orientation (100 % valid in predictable blocks vs 50 % in neutral blocks). With a perceptual-load 1-back letter task at fixation (grating task-irrelevant, spatially unattended) ES was present: V1 t32 = 2.90, p = 0.0068; V2 t32 = 2.54, p = 0.016. With a working-memory 2-back colour task at fixation: no ES (V1 t32 = −0.94; V2 t32 = −1.17). When the grating itself was the task (spatial-frequency discrimination, attended): no ES (V1 t32 = −1.52, p = 0.14; V2 t32 = 0.26). Perceptual vs WM task interaction V1 F(1,32) = 5.08, p = 0.031; perceptual vs grating task V1 F = 7.02, p = 0.012. — [PMC4476778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4476778/)
- Ekman, Kok & de Lange 2017, "Time-compressed preplay of anticipated events in human primary visual cortex", *Nat Commun* 8:15276 (N = 29, ultra-fast fMRI TR = 88 ms): after 108 training trials of a 4-dot spatial sequence (−10° to +10°, 2° above fixation), flashing only the first dot produced a V1 activity wave over the sequence's receptive-field locations, compressed ~2.2× (attended TCF 2.22 ± 0.23, t(28) = 6.45; unattended 2.29 ± 0.34, t(28) = 3.83). With attention diverted to an X/Y letter-detection RSVP at fixation, stimulation and preplay BOLD fell by the same proportion (53 % vs 51 %; t(28) = 1.22, P = 0.31), i.e. preplay "remains present even when attention is diverted from the stimulus sequence". — [PMC5457495](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5457495/)
- den Ouden, Friston, Daw, McIntosh & Stephan 2009, "A dual role for prediction error in associative learning", *Cereb Cortex* 19, 1175–1185 — cited by Richter & de Lange 2019 as a report of ES for task-irrelevant stimuli without a competing task (abstract-level here). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhn161)

**Macaque statistical-learning studies with passive viewing (relevant because the predicted stimuli were never task-relevant)**
- Meyer & Olson 2011, *PNAS* 108, 19401–19406: after prolonged exposure to fixed image pairs, IT neurons "exhibited a transitional surprise effect, responding much more strongly to unpredicted transitions than to predicted transitions" (abstract-level; monkeys viewed passively). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1112895108)
- Feuerriegel, Vogels & Kovács 2021 (review) conclude ES was "consistently found in a subset of statistical learning designs, involving long-term training periods" in macaque IT (Ramachandran et al. 2017; Kaposvari, Kumar & Vogels 2018 *Cereb Cortex* 28, 250–266; Vergnieux & Vogels 2020), whereas with probabilities learned in one or two sessions "evidence for ES was inconsistent". — [preprint PDF](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639); published as *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 126, 368–381, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.04.002)

**Replication status**
- I found no published direct replication of Richter & de Lange 2019's attended-vs-unattended contrast. A Radboud master's thesis titled "Expectation suppression depends on working memory but not perceptual load" exists in the Radboud thesis repository, but its content was not retrieved (UNVERIFIED as to results). — [Radboud theses listing](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/items/6f1d3ccf-636f-4dbb-9849-70c9f33480a8)
- Feuerriegel et al. 2021 treat Richter & de Lange 2019 and Larsson & Smith 2012 as mutually supportive: "[surprising – expected] BOLD signal differences when participants attended to the critical stimuli, but not when participants were required to ignore these stimuli and concurrently perform a difficult task (for similar findings see Larsson & Smith, 2012)". They add that visual mismatch responses "are also attenuated when spatial attention is directed away from the critical stimuli (File & Czigler, 2019; Smout et al., 2019, 2020)". — [preprint PDF](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639)
- The same review notes that ES has NOT replicated in many designs irrespective of attention: "Of the small number of experiments that included neutral conditions, only a minority reported statistically significant effects of ES, and a larger number of studies have produced data that are more consistent with a null effect", and "[surprising – expected] differences observed across fMRI experiments do not appear to replicate when analysing electrophysiological measures". — [preprint PDF](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639)
- Two recent EEG preprints report little or no ES from probabilistic cueing even with attended gratings: den Ouden, Kashyap, Kikkawa & Feuerriegel 2024 (N = 48; cue validities 10–90 %; "We did not observe any stimulus expectancy effects on grating-evoked event-related potentials") — [bioRxiv](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.05.26.595980); Hu, Tran & Rideaux 2025 ("evidence for visual expectancy was limited"; neither motor nor visual expectancy modulated representational fidelity) — [bioRxiv](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.03.22.644773). Both are preprints (not peer-reviewed at time of checking).

### Inferences
- The disagreement between Richter/Larsson (no ES unattended) and Kok 2012/St. John-Saaltink 2015/Ekman 2017 (effects present unattended) tracks stimulus complexity and prediction type: learned object/face associations (needing high-level identity processing) lose their expectation effect when attention is withdrawn; cued spatial/orientation predictions and over-learned spatial sequences in V1 do not. Richter & de Lange themselves flag this boundary.
- St. John-Saaltink 2015 undermines the "competing task" explanation that Richter & de Lange give for prior positive results: a competing perceptual-load task at fixation did NOT abolish ES for peripheral gratings, whereas a WM-load task did. Whether a prediction survives inattention may depend on what resource the competing task consumes (template maintenance), not on spatial attention per se.
- For the architecture question: the evidence allows prediction to be generated for unattended locations in early visual cortex (Kok 2012 V1; Ekman 2017 V1 preplay), but the high-level "what-comes-next" identity prediction that drives ES throughout the ventral stream appears to require the stimulus to be inside attention. A design where cross-level identity expectations live only inside attended regions, while low-level location/sequence expectations are computed field-wide, is consistent with the data; a design with no prediction at all outside attention is contradicted by Kok 2012 and Ekman 2017.

### Gaps
- No independent, published replication of Richter & de Lange 2019 with a different stimulus class or with a competing task of matched load was found.
- Larsson & Smith 2012 had N = 8; their null for unattended expectation is low-powered (they acknowledge sensitivity loss under inattention).
- I could not retrieve the full text of den Ouden et al. 2009 to report its attention conditions.
- No study was found that manipulated attention parametrically (graded load) to test whether ES falls off continuously or switches off.

## Q2. Kok, Rahnev, Jehee, Lau & de Lange 2012 (Cereb Cortex): what was found, and how has it held up?

### Takeaway
Kok et al. 2012 reported an attention × prediction interaction in human V1–V3: spatially predicted gratings evoked LESS V1 activity than unpredicted ones when unattended, but MORE when attended ("attention reverses the effect of prediction"), with omission responses only in the attended hemifield. The interaction has not been directly replicated with fMRI; time-resolved EEG (Alilović et al. 2019) found no such interaction before ~200 ms and only late (P3a), and several reviews treat the design (no neutral condition; possible attentional capture by unpredicted stimuli) as confounded. Other EEG work finds mixed additive/interactive patterns.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Kok, P., Rahnev, D., Jehee, J. F. M., Lau, H. C., & de Lange, F. P. (2012). Attention reverses the effect of prediction in silencing sensory signals. *Cerebral Cortex*, 22(9), 2197–2206. doi 10.1093/cercor/bhr310. — [SKKU record with abstract](https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/attention-reverses-the-effect-of-prediction-in-silencing-sensory-/); [PDF](https://rahnevlab.gatech.edu/documents/papers/Kok,Rahnev,Jehee,Lau,deLange(2012)CerebCortex.pdf)
- Design (from the PDF): 22 recruited, 17 analysed (3 excluded for head motion, 2 for not understanding the cues). Block-wise prediction cue (left/right/neutral; 75 % likelihood of the cued hemifield, blocks of 8 trials); trial-wise attention cue indicating the task-relevant hemifield (100 % valid for relevance; no predictive value for location). Gratings flashed 50 ms left or right, near-horizontal (95°) or near-vertical (5°); orientation identification only for attended-side stimuli. Response rates 95.5 % (attended) vs 2.6 % (unattended), unaffected by the prediction cue (P > 0.10). Accuracy 87.3 % not altered by prediction; RT faster for predicted (806 ms) than unpredicted (850 ms; t16 = 3.164, P = 0.006) and neutral (839 ms; t16 = 4.220). Eye movements did not differ across conditions. — [PDF](https://rahnevlab.gatech.edu/documents/papers/Kok,Rahnev,Jehee,Lau,deLange(2012)CerebCortex.pdf)
- Results: unattended side, predicted < unpredicted in V1 (t16 = 2.40, P = 0.029), not significant in V2 (t16 = 1.55, P = 0.139) or V3 (t16 = 1.01, P = 0.327). Attended side, predicted > unpredicted: V1 t16 = 2.25, P = 0.039; V2 t16 = 1.96, P = 0.067; V3 t16 = 2.67, P = 0.017. The response to UNpredicted stimuli did not differ between attended and unattended. Unpredicted omission of a stimulus in the attended field evoked more activity than predicted omission (V1 t16 = 2.99, P = 0.009; V2 t16 = 2.79, P = 0.013; V3 t16 = 2.90, P = 0.010); no omission effect in the unattended field (V1 t16 = 1.52, P = 0.148; V2 t16 = 1.25; V3 t16 = 0.96). No effects in stimulus-unresponsive voxels (all P > 0.10). DCM: without attention prediction modulated V1's self-connection negatively (faster decay, "faster resolution of prediction error"); with attention the modulation was positive and forward connections V1→V2/V3 strengthened. — [PDF](https://rahnevlab.gatech.edu/documents/papers/Kok,Rahnev,Jehee,Lau,deLange(2012)CerebCortex.pdf)
- Interpretation: "attention increases the postsynaptic gain of only those prediction error neurons that receive their inputs from neurons that are 'primed' by the current predictions". The authors concede an alternative in terms of "enhancement of relevant signals and suppression of distractors" and state "we cannot rule out such an interpretation on the basis of the current data alone". — [PDF](https://rahnevlab.gatech.edu/documents/papers/Kok,Rahnev,Jehee,Lau,deLange(2012)CerebCortex.pdf)

How it has held up:
- Alilović, Timmermans, Reteig, van Gaal & Slagter 2019, "No evidence that predictions and attention modulate the first feedforward sweep of cortical information processing", *Cerebral Cortex* (2 EEG experiments, N = 21 and N = 13; orthogonal spatial prediction 75/50/25 % and trial-wise attention): no attention or prediction effect on C1 (50–80 ms) — Exp. 1 Attention F(1,18) = 0.079, Prediction F(2,17) = 0.608, interaction F(2,17) = 0.173; BF01 = 6.6 (attention), 18.1 (prediction), BFexclusion = 10.9 (interaction); Exp. 2 BF01 = 4.95 / 6.99 / 8.0. P1 (100–150 ms): attention enhancement (Attention × Hemisphere F(1,19) = 7.221, p = 0.015), prediction marginal (p = 0.065), interaction n.s. (F = 0.317, p = 0.732). N1: attention F(1,19) = 22.431, p < 0.001; prediction n.s.; interaction marginal (F(2,18) = 3.311, p = 0.06). P3a: both reduced amplitude, interaction F(2,18) = 8.831, p < 0.001, with the prediction effect LARGER when unattended. P3b: prediction F(2,18) = 32.283, p < 0.001; attention n.s. MVPA: attention decodable from ~133 ms, prediction from ~242 ms, nothing before 80 ms. Authors argue fMRI V1 effects (Kok 2012) likely reflect feedback, not feedforward filtering. — [PMC6484894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6484894)
- Jiang, Summerfield & Egner 2013 found the attention × expectation interaction only multivariately (see Q6) and report that for the same conditions "no attention by expectation interaction effect of the kind we detected in the multivariate analyses was observed" in univariate data. — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- St. John-Saaltink et al. 2015 found no ES for attended gratings and cite Kok 2012 as a possible reason ("attention interacts with expectation and may even reverse its effect"). — [PMC4476778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4476778/)
- Marzecová, Schettino, Widmann, SanMiguel, Kotz & Schröger 2018 (*Sci Rep* 8, doi 10.1038/s41598-017-18347-1; N = 18): block-wise spatial attention × 75/25 % orientation expectation (orientation task-irrelevant; spatial-frequency task at the attended side). N1pc: additive (model with two main effects preferred, BF10 = 33,230 vs. interaction model); anterior N1a: attention effect "observed only for the gratings of predicted orientation", which the authors relate to Kok 2012 and precision. — [PMC5758810](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5758810/)
- Smout, Tang, Garrido & Mattingley 2019 (EEG, *PLoS Biol* 17(2): e2006812, N = 24) found attention selectively enhanced mismatch (prediction-error) information for deviants in the 332–480 ms window (cluster p = 0.031), while attention raised orientation-selective amplitude independent of prediction (219–550 ms, p < 0.001) — an interaction on PE encoding, not a reversal of a suppression. They cite Kok 2012 as prior evidence that attention can reverse prediction's suppressive effect. — [PMC6411367](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6411367)
- Garrido, Rowe, Halász & Mattingley 2018, "Bayesian mapping reveals that attention boosts neural responses to predicted and unpredicted stimuli", *Cereb Cortex* 28, 1771–1782 (auditory; abstract-level): attention boosted responses to both predicted and unpredicted tones — additive, no reversal. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhx087)
- Feuerriegel et al. 2021 classify Kok 2012a with designs that "presented only expected and surprising stimulus conditions", so it is "unclear whether the observed [surprising – expected] differences are due to ES, surprise-related responses, or a mixture". — [preprint PDF](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639)
- Rungratsameetaweemana & Serences 2019 (*Curr Opin Psychol* 29, 181–186) list Kok 2012 among studies whose expectation cues "inherently signaled task relevance" or otherwise conflated expectation with attention. — [PMC6756985](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6756985)
- Alink & Blank 2021, "Can expectation suppression be explained by reduced attention to predictable stimuli?", *NeuroImage* 231, 117824: propose that "reduced responses to predictable stimuli can also be explained by a reduced saliency-driven allocation of attention" and that resolving this "requires the assessment of qualitative differences between the ways in which attention and 'surprise' enhance brain responses" (abstract; via Crossref). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.117824); [bioRxiv abstract](https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.06.19.154427)

### Inferences
- The "unattended suppression" half of Kok 2012 (V1 only, t = 2.40, N = 17, no neutral baseline) is a small effect by today's standards, and the "attended enhancement" half can be read as attention × expectation synergy or as suppression of distractors at the unexpected location; the authors say so. No later fMRI study has reproduced the full crossover.
- Time-resolved data (Alilović 2019; Marzecová 2018; Smout 2019) put any attention × prediction interaction at ≥150–200 ms, often ≥300 ms, consistent with feedback/recurrent processing rather than gating of afferent input. Design-relevant reading: the interaction is late and post-perceptual, not a property of the first pass.

### Gaps
- No direct fMRI replication of Kok 2012 with a neutral condition and with attention actively captured by a competing stimulus.
- Effect sizes in standardized units are not reported in the 2012 paper; only t-values with N = 17.

## Q3. Summerfield & Egner 2009 and 2016 (TiCS): the proposed dissociation and its evidence

### Takeaway
Summerfield & Egner define attention as prioritisation by motivational/task relevance and expectation as constraint by prior likelihood; in 2016 they argue feature-based attention (FBA) and feature-based expectation (FBE) are computationally distinct because an ideal observer should weight relevant evidence (gain → sensitivity) but only shift its criterion for probable evidence, so any sensitivity gain from expectation requires a different mechanism (sharpening / "off-channel" sensitivity). Their neural evidence for expectation is mainly fMRI expectation suppression (Summerfield et al. 2008; Egner et al. 2010; Kok et al. 2012), which later reviews consider confounded.

### Cited Findings
- Summerfield, C., & Egner, T. (2009). Expectation (and attention) in visual cognition. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 13(9), 403–409. doi 10.1016/j.tics.2009.06.003. Abstract: "attention prioritizes stimulus processing on the basis of motivational relevance, and expectations constrain visual interpretation on the basis of prior likelihood ... we review recent work that has begun to delineate a neurobiology of visual expectation, and contrast the findings with those of the attention literature, to explore how these two central influences on visual perception overlap, differ and interact." (abstract-level; full text not accessible) — [Europe PMC abstract via Crossref DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2009.06.003)
- The 2009 review's core empirical plank is Summerfield, Trittschuh, Monti, Mesulam & Egner 2008, "Neural repetition suppression reflects fulfilled perceptual expectations", *Nature Neuroscience* 11, 1004–1006: fMRI repetition suppression (FFA) was reduced when repetitions were improbable, read as "a relative reduction in top-down perceptual 'prediction error'". — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.2163)
- Egner, Monti & Summerfield 2010, "Expectation and surprise determine neural population responses in the ventral visual stream", *J Neurosci* 30, 16601–16608: FFA responses modelled as a sum of face-expectation and face-surprise components rather than feature detection (abstract-level). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.2770-10.2010)
- Summerfield, C., & Egner, T. (2016). Feature-based attention and feature-based expectation. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 20(6), 401–404. FBA "prioritizes processing by providing information about feature relevance"; FBE provides "information about signal probability, but not relevance". Ideal-observer argument: "knowledge of stimulus probabilities should not enhance discrimination sensitivity. Rather, performance increases because the ideal observer adjusts the decision criterion", whereas for relevance "one motion direction becomes more relevant than others, and thus can be given more weight in the decision". Evidence cited: Kok et al. 2012 (expected orientation → lower BOLD in early visual cortex, "suggestive of sharper population tuning"); Wyart et al. 2012 (FBE advantage strongest at low signal energy); Cheadle et al. 2015 (FBE increased "sensitivity to more diagnostic, 'off-channel' features" versus FBA's "multiplicative influence"). The paper does not address whether expectation operates without attention. — [PMC4875850](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4875850)
- Cheadle, Egner, Wyart, Wu & Summerfield 2015, "Feature expectation heightens visual sensitivity during fine orientation discrimination", *J Vision* 15(14):14 — the FBE-sensitivity finding (verified citation; abstract-level). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/15.14.14)
- The same lineage's earlier dissociation claim: Summerfield & de Lange 2014, "Expectation in perceptual decision making: neural and computational mechanisms", *Nat Rev Neurosci* 15, 745–756 (review; cited by Feuerriegel 2021 and Rungratsameetaweemana & Serences 2019 for the relevance-vs-probability definition). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn3838)
- Critique: Larsson & Smith 2012 reinterpret Summerfield 2008 as attention-dependent ("subjects might attend more strongly to infrequent trials") and show the effect disappears with diverted attention. — [PMC3278317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278317)
- Critique: in macaque IT, face repetition probability did not affect repetition suppression (Vinken, Op de Beeck & Vogels 2018, *J Neurosci* 38, 7492–7504; verified citation, abstract-level), and Grotheer & Kovács 2015 (*Neuropsychologia* 67, 175–182) found additive rather than interactive effects of repetition and expectation (as summarised by Feuerriegel 2021). — [Vinken DOI](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.0462-18.2018); [Feuerriegel preprint](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639)

### Inferences
- The 2016 framework is normative: relevance should change gain (d′), probability should change criterion (c). Empirical deviations (expectation raising sensitivity: Wyart 2012 energy-sensitivity of false alarms; Cheadle 2015; Stein & Peelen 2015) are what the authors use to argue that FBE is implemented by a distinct, template-like mechanism rather than by gain.
- The 2009 review's fMRI evidence for expectation (repetition-probability effects) is exactly the evidence that is attention-dependent (Larsson & Smith 2012) and inconsistently replicated (Vinken 2018; Feuerriegel 2021). The dissociation claim is more secure at the psychophysical/computational level (Q4) than at the neural level.

### Gaps
- Full text of Summerfield & Egner 2009 was not retrievable; specific figures/claims beyond the abstract are not reported here.

## Q4. Wyart, Nobre & Summerfield 2012 (PNAS): probability shifts bias, relevance changes sensitivity

### Takeaway
With N = 10 and a reverse-correlation design that cued signal probability (block level) and relevance (trial level) independently at two locations, relevance increased d′ (and the energy sensitivity of hits, modelled as internal-noise suppression / response gain), whereas probability lowered the criterion without changing d′ but increased the frequency AND energy sensitivity of false alarms, modelled as an additive "input bias" in signal-selective units (+3.6 % for cued-present, −1.6 % for cued-absent). Expectation therefore acts like a baseline shift upstream of the decision, not a mere response-criterion shift, and it is separable from attention's gain.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Wyart, V., Nobre, A. C., & Summerfield, C. (2012). Dissociable prior influences of signal probability and relevance on visual contrast sensitivity. *PNAS*, 109(9), 3593–3598. doi 10.1073/pnas.1120118109. A correction notice was also published in PNAS 2012 (PMC3341032; content not retrieved). — [PMC3295248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3295248/); [ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:dce8003b-eaae-4952-b1e4-d2cba48a9f26)
- Design: 10 subjects (19–28 y, six female). Vertical Gabor (2 cpd) added to Gaussian noise, 4° diameter, centred 4° left and right of fixation. Probability cued "at the block level using a cue indicating the prior probability of signal occurrence in each of the two colored placeholders"; relevance cued "at the trial level using a prestimulus cue indicating the most likely color of the poststimulus probe". — [PMC3295248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3295248/)
- Conventional SDT: relevance increased d′ (F2,18 = 23.4, P < 0.001; cued-relevant up, F1,9 = 19.8, P = 0.001; cued-irrelevant down, F1,9 = 15.5, P < 0.005, "matching known effects of spatial attention on visual contrast sensitivity"); probability lowered bias c (F2,18 = 10.2, P = 0.001) but did not increase d′ (F2,18 < 1, P > 0.5); stimuli cued as likely had a higher false-alarm rate (F1,9 = 10.0, P = 0.01). Figure legend: "Signal probability biases detection judgments by increasing hit and false-alarm rates to a similar extent" while "Signal relevance improves the precision of signal processing by selectively decreasing the false-alarm rate for cued stimuli". — [PMC3295248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3295248/)
- Reverse correlation: probability increased the energy sensitivity of false alarms (interaction F1,9 = 5.2, P < 0.05; cued-present F1,9 = 16.4, P < 0.005) but not of hits (F1,9 < 1); relevance increased the energy sensitivity of hits (interaction F1,9 = 16.5, P < 0.005; cued-relevant F1,9 = 180.7, P < 0.001) but not of false alarms (F1,9 = 1.7). Four-way interaction cue type × cue content × stimulus category × signal energy, F1,9 = 5.3, P < 0.05. — [PMC3295248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3295248/)
- Modelling: probability effects best fit by an "input bias" model (additive δ: +3.6 % cued-present, −1.6 % cued-absent; χ² = 20.5 vs null, P < 0.001), which reproduced both c and energy sensitivity (F1,9 < 1); a pure "response bias" (criterion θ) model fit c but was "formally rejected" for energy sensitivity (F1,9 = 12.5, P < 0.01); a "response gain" (σ) model did not beat null for probability. Relevance effects were best fit by the response-gain model (internal-noise suppression). Abstract: relevance "increased primarily the sensitivity of true positives by suppressing internal noise during signal processing, whereas cues predicting greater signal probability increased both the frequency and the sensitivity of false positives by biasing the baseline activity of signal-selective units." — [PMC3295248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3295248/)

Related psychophysics on criterion vs sensitivity:
- Bang & Rahnev 2017, "Stimulus expectation alters decision criterion but not sensory signal in perceptual decision making", *Sci Rep* 7 (doi 10.1038/s41598-017-16885-2): post-stimulus cues had a LARGER effect on criterion c than pre-stimulus cues; pre- and post-cues had comparable effects on sensitivity and on the use of information over time, so expectation's effect was placed at the decision stage. — [Crossref abstract](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-16885-2)
- Rahnev, Maniscalco, Graves, Huang, de Lange & Lau 2011, "Attention induces conservative subjective biases in visual perception", *Nat Neurosci* 14, 1513–1515 (verified citation; abstract-level): attention also moves the criterion (more conservative), so criterion shift is not an exclusive marker of expectation. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2948)
- Stein & Peelen 2015, "Content-specific expectations enhance stimulus detectability by increasing perceptual sensitivity", *JEP: General* 144, 1089–1104 (verified citation; abstract-level): expectation increased d′ — a counter-example to the pure-criterion account. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000109)
- Rungratsameetaweemana, Itthipuripat, Salazar & Serences 2018, "Expectations do not alter early sensory processing during perceptual decision-making", *J Neurosci* 38, 5632–5648: feature expectations induced by stimulus history (no explicit cue) improved speed/accuracy but did not modulate early sensory EEG markers; effects appeared in posterior alpha and frontal theta (summarised in their 2019 review). — [PMC6756985](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6756985)

### Inferences
- Wyart 2012's "input bias" is a baseline offset in signal-selective units at the expected location — mechanistically a located, additive prior that fires false alarms when noise resembles the signal. This is the psychophysical signature closest to "expectation = predicted image added to (or compared with) the input" in the architecture; attention's signature is multiplicative/noise-suppressive. The two signatures are separable in one task with the same stimuli.
- Importantly, Wyart cued probability for a location that could be relevant or irrelevant on a given trial; the bias effect existed at both. This is behavioural evidence that a spatial probability prior operates at a location that is not currently the relevant one (within the same two-location display, not a demanding competing task).

### Gaps
- The published correction to Wyart et al. 2012 was not retrieved; I cannot say which numbers it changed.
- N = 10 and no replication was located in this session.

## Q5. Reviews 2015–2021: consensus on where/when expectation acts and whether it needs attention

### Takeaway
There is no consensus that expectation requires attention. The reviews agree on (i) a terminological separation (attention = relevance/salience, expectation = probability), (ii) the fact that most paradigms confound them, (iii) that effects attributed to expectation in V1 are late/feedback-related rather than in the first ~80 ms, and (iv) that "expectation suppression" is weakly replicated outside long-training statistical-learning designs. The de Lange/Kok camp holds that expectation effects on sensory representations are "relatively automatic" (templates form regardless of feature relevance) yet gated by spatial/object attention; the Serences camp holds that expectation mostly acts at decisional stages; Press, Kok & Yon argue that a late, surprise-triggered upweighting of unexpected input exists and should be tested against task relevance.

### Cited Findings
- de Lange, F. P., Heilbron, M., & Kok, P. (2018). How do expectations shape perception? *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 22(9), 764–779. doi 10.1016/j.tics.2018.06.002. Abstract-level only (full text behind paywalls/captcha): "perception and perceptual decision-making are strongly facilitated by prior knowledge about the probabilistic structure of the world ... there are myriad ways in which this computation can be realized." — [UCL Discovery record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10056291/)
- Kok, Mostert & de Lange 2017, "Prior expectations induce prestimulus sensory templates", *PNAS* 114(39), 10473–10478 (MEG, N = 23; 75 %-valid auditory cue for orientation; orientation vs contrast task): expectation-induced orientation signals appeared ~40 ms before stimulus onset; "there was no interaction between the effects of the expectation cue and the task", so templates formed whether or not orientation was task-relevant; template strength correlated with behaviour only in the orientation task (r = 0.44, p = 0.035 vs r = −0.13, p = 0.55). Authors: "expectations may be a relatively automatic phenomenon". — [PMC5625909](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5625909)
- Press, C., Kok, P., & Yon, D. (2020). The perceptual prediction paradox. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 24(1), 13–24. Two-process ("Opposing Process") proposal: perception "is first biased towards prior knowledge ... operational from the point at which predictions can be made, that is, often before an event is presented, through preactivation of expected units", and "when an event generates high surprise ... later processes are triggered (after event presentation) which highlight these events", on "the timescale of hundreds of milliseconds within a single event", via phasic catecholamine release raising sensory gain and saccades toward surprising events. On attention: "recent work aiming to differentiate these concepts considers attentional mechanisms to be only those that highlight task-relevant input"; "many apparent effects of probability could be the result of confounded task-relevance"; "under our theory mechanisms generating upweighting of the expected and unexpected rely on probabilistic information, not information about task-relevance, but empirical efforts must examine whether the operation of these mechanisms is altered when task relevance is manipulated." The review cites Richter & de Lange 2019, Kok 2012 and Alilović 2019 in this context. — [accepted manuscript PDF](https://research.gold.ac.uk/28217/1/PredParadox%20TICS.pdf); [Goldsmiths record](https://research.gold.ac.uk/28217)
- Feuerriegel, D., Vogels, R., & Kovács, G. (2021). Evaluating the evidence for expectation suppression in the visual system. *Neurosci Biobehav Rev*, 126, 368–381. Four confounds: surprise, attention, repetition/adaptation, novelty. On attention: "Effects of attention may confound measures of ES if attention is rapidly directed to a stimulus that violates an observer's expectations", supported by Richter 2019 and Larsson & Smith 2012; "some predictive coding-based accounts propose that ES should indeed be most pronounced for attended stimuli (e.g., Auksztulewicz & Friston, 2016)"; proposed discriminator (after Alink & Blank 2021): attention-capture accounts predict [surprising − expected] differences EARLIER than task-relevance attention effects, prediction accounts predict them LATER; "There is some evidence that task-related attention has an earlier influence than expectation-related stimulus-evoked response modulations". Verdict: "If ES is instead interpreted as the absence of a surprise-related response ... then there appears to be substantial evidence for such an effect. In this case, the term 'expectation suppression' is arguably a misnomer". — [preprint PDF](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639); [published DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.04.002)
- Alilović et al. 2019 (*Cereb Cortex*): "initial visual afferent activity may be impenetrable to top-down influences by spatial prediction and attention"; pre-stimulus alpha showed that both cues were used, yet no evoked modulation before 80 ms; attention effects from P1 (100–150 ms), prediction effects robust only at P3a/P3b (~300–450 ms); MVPA attention from ~133 ms, prediction from ~242 ms. — [PMC6484894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6484894)
- Rungratsameetaweemana & Serences 2019 (*Curr Opin Psychol* 29, 181–186): define attention as "the mechanism that operates based on the behavioral relevance of different stimuli" and expectation as "the mechanism that operates based on the probability of stimulus occurrence"; argue Posner-type cues confound them; conclude that "knowledge about statistical regularities of the sensory environment primarily influence later cognitive operations related to response selection and execution". They do not claim expectation requires attention, only that most designs cannot attribute early effects to expectation alone. — [PMC6756985](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6756985)
- Yon, de Lange & Press 2019, "The predictive brain as a stubborn scientist", *TiCS* 23, 6–8 (verified citation): the "stubborn priors" idea Richter & de Lange invoke for predictions that might survive inattention. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2018.10.003)
- Walsh, McGovern, Clark & O'Connell 2020, "Evaluating the neurophysiological evidence for predictive processing as a model of perception", *Ann N Y Acad Sci* 1464, 242–268 (verified; abstract-level): a critical review of PP's empirical support. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.14321)
- Aitchison & Lengyel 2017, "With or without you: predictive coding and Bayesian inference in the brain", *Curr Opin Neurobiol* 46, 219–227 (verified; abstract-level): separates predictive coding (an algorithm with error units) from Bayesian inference (a computational goal) — relevant to whether "suppression" must be observed at all. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conb.2017.08.010)
- Richter, Heilbron & de Lange 2022, "Dampened sensory representations for expected input across the ventral visual stream", *Oxford Open Neuroscience* 1, kvac013 (abstract): contrasts sharpening (populations tuned away suppressed) with dampening (populations tuned toward the expected stimulus suppressed); "Empirical support is div[ided]". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/oons/kvac013)
- Richter, Kietzmann & de Lange 2024, "High-level visual prediction errors in early visual cortex", *PLoS Biol* 22: e3002829 (abstract): prediction errors in both low- and high-level visual cortex responded to HIGH-level (DNN-defined) surprise, consistent with a surprise signal computed at higher levels and broadcast down rather than local mismatch at each level. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002829)
- Audition contrast (brief): Heilbron & Chait 2018, "Great expectations: is there evidence for predictive coding in auditory cortex?", *Neuroscience* 389, 54–73 (verified citation) reviews the auditory case, where MMN to task-irrelevant deviants is routinely recorded under inattention — covered by another researcher. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2017.07.061)

### Inferences
- Across reviews the empirically safest statements are: (a) feedforward V1 activity (< 80 ms) is not modulated by either factor; (b) attention modulates evoked activity from ~100 ms; (c) expectation modulates late components (≥ 240–300 ms) robustly and early components weakly; (d) ES as a suppression relative to NEUTRAL is poorly replicated except after weeks of statistical learning; (e) ES as a surprise response (unexpected > expected) is robust when the stimulus is attended, and reduced or absent when it is not (Richter 2019; Larsson & Smith 2012; File & Czigler 2019; Smout 2019).
- The reviews converge on an operational test the architecture could mirror: whether a given "surprise" signal is an orienting/attention-capture response (fast, salience-like) or a prediction-error computation (later, depends on the learned model). The present evidence cannot yet tell them apart in humans.

### Gaps
- Full texts of de Lange et al. 2018 and Summerfield & Egner 2009 were not accessible; their Box-level treatment of attention could not be quoted.
- No meta-analysis of ES effect sizes as a function of attention was found.

## Q6. Jiang, Summerfield & Egner 2013 (J Neurosci): attention sharpens the distinction between expected and unexpected percepts

### Takeaway
With a 75 %-valid auditory cue for face vs scene and a block-wise target-category task, cross-subject MVPA in FFA/PPA distinguished expected from unexpected stimuli at 75 % accuracy (z = 3.2) when the category was attended but at chance when unattended; the authors take this as support for the "PE-promotion" model (attention raises the precision/gain of prediction errors) over "PE-suppression". The interaction was absent in univariate BOLD, and the chance-level result for unattended stimuli is attributed by the authors partly to between-subject variance.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Jiang, J., Summerfield, C., & Egner, T. (2013). Attention sharpens the distinction between expected and unexpected percepts in the visual brain. *Journal of Neuroscience*, 33(47), 18438–18447. — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- Design: 21 participants (7 male, mean 25 y). Four stimulus types (male/female faces, indoor/outdoor scenes; 60 images each). Rising/falling tone cue → 75 % probability of face or scene. Attention: rare-target detection, with one stimulus type designated target per block (e.g., female-face block: male faces = attended non-targets; scenes = unattended). — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- Univariate: expected stimuli showed enhanced category selectivity in FFA/PPA; attended stimuli showed stronger selectivity than unattended; but "no attention by expectation interaction effect of the kind we detected in the multivariate analyses was observed". — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- MVPA (between-subject classifier): expected vs unexpected discrimination for attended faces in FFA rose "from chance (50% accuracy, z = 0) to 75% (z = 3.2, above chance at p < 0.001)"; same for attended scenes in PPA (75 %, z = 3.2). "Classifiers could neither distinguish expected from unexpected stimuli at above chance levels in the unattended condition." — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- Models: PE-suppression (attention filters out unexpected information → patterns more similar) vs PE-promotion (attention raises PE gain → patterns more distinct). Findings "strongly support the PE-promotion model, whereby attention increases the precision of prediction errors"; "attention enhances the precision (inverse of variance) of PEs by increasing the gain of error processing in a multiplicative fashion". — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- Relation to the study Jiang et al. cite as Kok et al. 2012a (as summarised from their discussion; the specific Kok paper meant was not independently checked here): that study decoded stimulus identity in V1 and found non-interacting benefits of attention and expectation, whereas Jiang decoded expectation-violation status; the authors reconcile them as answering different questions. — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- Limitations stated: chance performance for unattended may reflect "large degree of variance in error signaling across subjects when stimuli are unattended"; between-subject MVPA gives one accuracy per condition (Bayesian testing used); design "suboptimal for within-subject MVPA"; mean univariate signals "can obscure interactions". — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- Feuerriegel 2021 lists Jiang 2013 among studies in which stimulus-specific multivariate representations were enhanced for surprising vs expected stimuli, which "attention-based accounts of surprise responses predict", observed "in most (e.g., Jiang et al., 2013; Richter et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2018) but not all (e.g., Kok et al., 2012b; Yon et al., 2018)" studies. — [preprint PDF](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639)

### Inferences
- Jiang 2013 is the most direct human evidence that the NEURAL DISTINCTNESS of expected vs unexpected (a prediction-error-like code) depends on attention, but it is an absence of decodability with a between-subject classifier, not Bayesian evidence for a null, and the authors themselves hedge.
- Its interpretation (attention = precision of PE) is the same as Kok 2012's, so the two are not independent confirmations of a mechanism — both rest on Feldman & Friston 2010.

### Gaps
- No within-subject replication of the expected/unexpected decoding interaction was located.

## Q7. Are prediction ERROR signals (not just suppression) absent or weak without attention in vision?

### Takeaway
Weak but present, not absent, is the best summary. EEG mismatch (deviant − control) responses to ignored gratings exist but are much smaller and lack the attention-enhanced late component (Smout 2019); vMMN to task-irrelevant peripheral deviants is reliably elicited under a central task but shrinks with distance from the attended field (File & Czigler 2019; Stefanics 2014 recommends a demanding central task to isolate it); fMRI omission responses appeared only in the attended hemifield (Kok 2012); late P3a surprise effects were larger for unattended stimuli (Alilović 2019); whereas for complex objects the expected-vs-unexpected BOLD difference vanished with diverted attention (Richter 2019; Jiang 2013 for decodability). In audition, MMN under inattention is the classic case of PE outside attention, and attention enhances rather than creates it.

### Cited Findings
- Smout, Tang, Garrido & Mattingley 2019, "Attention promotes the neural encoding of prediction errors", *PLoS Biology* 17(2): e2006812 (N = 24; roving-oddball gratings, 9 orientations, 100 ms, 500 ms ISI; attended = grating spatial-frequency task; ignored = fixation-dot contrast task, sessions ~1 week apart, both ~75 % detection): mismatch responses "were clearly weaker in response to controls and ignored deviants" than attended deviants; mismatch main effect of prediction (deviant > control) 113–550 ms, p < 0.001; main effect of attention 188–550 ms, p = 0.002; attention × prediction interaction 332–480 ms, cluster p = 0.031 (attention enhanced deviant mismatch selectively); orientation-selective amplitude: deviants = controls (t[22] = 0.78, p = 0.443), standards smaller, attention raised orientation amplitude 219–550 ms independent of prediction. Authors: evidence that "attention optimises the expected precision of predictions by modulating the synaptic gain of prediction error units" (Feldman & Friston 2010); note that unattended mismatch responses "though present, were weak". — [PMC6411367](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6411367)
- File & Czigler 2019, "Automatic detection of violations of statistical regularities in the periphery is affected by the focus of spatial attention: a visual mismatch negativity study", *Eur J Neurosci* 49, 1348–1356: passive oddball (standard 45° p = 0.8, deviant 135° p = 0.2) plus equiprobable control at the lower screen while subjects tracked a bar; "Deviant stimuli in close proximity to a continuously attended field elicited larger vMMN than similar stimuli farther away"; nearer deviants were also followed by a posterior positivity. — [Crossref abstract](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejn.14306)
- Stefanics, Kremláček & Czigler 2014, "Visual mismatch negativity: a predictive coding view", *Front Hum Neurosci* 8:666: reviews protocols to control "refractoriness" (equiprobable controls) and "methods to control attention" for vMMN, treating vMMN as a perceptual prediction-error signal to task-irrelevant stimuli (abstract-level). — [PMC4165279](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4165279/)
- Kimura 2012, "Visual mismatch negativity and unintentional temporal-context-based prediction in vision", *Int J Psychophysiol* 83, 144–155 (verified citation only). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2011.11.010)
- Kok et al. 2012: omission of a predicted stimulus evoked more activity than omission of an unpredicted one only in the attended hemifield (V1 t16 = 2.99, P = 0.009), not the unattended (t16 = 1.52, P = 0.148); but the predicted-vs-unpredicted suppression for actual stimuli WAS present unattended in V1. — [Kok 2012 PDF](https://rahnevlab.gatech.edu/documents/papers/Kok,Rahnev,Jehee,Lau,deLange(2012)CerebCortex.pdf)
- Alilović et al. 2019: P3a was reduced by both factors with "Prediction effect larger in unattended vs. attended conditions" (interaction F(2,18) = 8.831, p < 0.001); P3b scaled inversely with probability regardless of attention (F(2,18) = 32.283; attention F(1,19) = 0.058). So a late surprise/novelty signal for unattended stimuli existed and was, if anything, bigger. — [PMC6484894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6484894)
- Richter & de Lange 2019: no expected-vs-unexpected difference anywhere for unattended objects (BF10 0.18–0.25), i.e. neither suppression nor surprise response; the authors also report (per Feuerriegel 2021) increased pupil dilation following surprising events. — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/); [Feuerriegel preprint](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639)
- Jiang 2013: expected/unexpected status not decodable for unattended categories (Q6). — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051)
- Tang, Smout, Arabzadeh & Mattingley 2018, "Prediction error and repetition suppression have distinct effects on neural representations of visual information", *eLife* 7: e33123 (attended gratings; verified citation, abstract-level): unexpected stimuli yielded enhanced orientation information relative to expected — the "surprise sharpens the representation" result Feuerriegel 2021 groups with attention-capture predictions. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.33123)
- Audition, for contrast only: Auksztulewicz & Friston 2015, "Attentional enhancement of auditory mismatch responses: a DCM/MEG study", *Cereb Cortex* 25, 4273–4283 (attention enhances the MMN); Chennu et al. 2013, "Expectation and attention in hierarchical auditory prediction", *J Neurosci* 33, 11194–11205; Garrido et al. 2018 (attention boosts predicted and unpredicted tones additively); Todorovic & de Lange 2012, *J Neurosci* 32, 13389–13395 (repetition suppression and expectation suppression dissociable in time in auditory evoked fields); Hsu, Hämäläinen & Waszak 2014, *Front Hum Neurosci* 8:152 (auditory N1 attended/predictable largest; sources in bilateral auditory areas). All verified citations; details not extracted here because MMN is covered by another researcher. — [Auksztulewicz DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhu323); [Chennu DOI](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.0114-13.2013); [Garrido DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhx087); [Todorovic DOI](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.2227-12.2012); [Hsu PMC3972470](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972470)

### Inferences
- A consistent pattern: low-level feature deviance (orientation oddball, location probability) generates a mismatch signal outside attention that attention amplifies, especially late (> 300 ms); high-level identity expectations (which object follows which) generate no measurable surprise signal when attention is withdrawn. Attention thus looks like a gain on PE (Feldman & Friston 2010 "precision") for simple features and like a gate on whether the PE is computed at all for complex, learned associations.
- Pupil dilation and P3a to unattended surprises indicate that the organism registers the violation even when the sensory-cortex ES signature is missing — i.e. a surprise/orienting signal can exist without a sensory-cortex prediction-error signature.

### Gaps
- No visual study measured PE for ignored stimuli with the sensitivity (Bayes factors, N ≥ 30) that Richter 2019 applied to ES.
- The equiprobable-control vMMN literature under strictly matched central load was not reviewed in depth (left to the MMN researcher).

## Q8. Are expectations spatially located (what-at-where) or feature/object-based and unlocated, and is spatial attention required to bind an expectation to a location?

### Takeaway
Both kinds exist and are separable in the data: spatial-probability expectations modulate retinotopic V1 (Kok 2012) and bias detection at a specific location even when that location is currently irrelevant (Wyart 2012); feature expectations evoke orientation templates at the attended location before stimulus onset irrespective of feature relevance (Kok 2017) and add to spatial-attention effects at unattended locations (Marzecová 2018); sequence predictions "preplay" at the correct receptive-field locations even when attention is at fixation (Ekman 2017). Spatial attention is not required to form or express a located expectation in V1, but it is required for object-identity expectations to show their sensory signature (Richter 2019), and statistical learning of which regularities exist is itself gated by selective attention (Turk-Browne 2005), while learned regularities in turn attract attention to their location/feature (Zhao 2013).

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The data separate three things the architecture conflates under "expectation": (1) a location prior (where something will appear) — behaves like Wyart's additive baseline and Kok 2012's retinotopic V1 modulation; present without spatial attention; (2) a feature template at a location (what orientation) — forms pre-stimulus regardless of feature relevance (Kok 2017), survives a central perceptual-load task (St. John-Saaltink 2015) but not a WM-load task; (3) an identity/transition prediction (which object next) — expressed in the ventral stream only when the object is attended (Richter 2019). The last is the level most like "predicted next word".
- Spatial attention is not what binds a prediction to a location in V1 (Ekman 2017 preplay lands at the right receptive fields with attention at fixation); but statistical learning of the regularity in the first place is gated by selective attention to the stream (Turk-Browne 2005), and once learned, regularities pull attention to their location and feature (Zhao 2013). A plausible loop: attention selects what gets learned; learned predictions then recruit attention; low-level located predictions are expressed field-wide, high-level ones only under attention.

### Gaps
- No study was found that dissociates "prediction of identity at an unattended location" from "prediction of identity for an unattended object at an attended location"; the spatial-vs-object-attention distinction Larsson & Smith flagged remains open.
- Turk-Browne 2005 volume/pages and the magnitude of unattended-stream learning were not captured (the full-text PDF returned 404).

## Interaction form: additive, multiplicative (gain on PE), or reversal — a cross-study tally

### Takeaway
No single interaction form holds. Reversal (attention flips suppression into enhancement) was reported once with fMRI (Kok 2012) and partially by St. John-Saaltink 2015's null for attended gratings; multiplicative gain on PE (interaction confined to the mismatch/expected-vs-unexpected signal) by Jiang 2013 and Smout 2019 (late, 332–480 ms); gating (effect present only if attended) by Richter 2019 and Larsson & Smith 2012; additive by Marzecová 2018 (N1pc), Garrido 2018 (auditory), Grotheer & Kovács 2015 (repetition × expectation), and Alilović 2019 (P1/N1, with a late P3a interaction in the OPPOSITE direction — prediction effect larger when unattended); dissociable mechanisms (bias vs gain) by Wyart 2012.

### Cited Findings
- Reversal: Kok et al. 2012 (Q2). — [PDF](https://rahnevlab.gatech.edu/documents/papers/Kok,Rahnev,Jehee,Lau,deLange(2012)CerebCortex.pdf)
- Gain on PE: Jiang 2013 (Q6); Smout 2019 (Q7). — [PMC3834051](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3834051); [PMC6411367](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6411367)
- Gating: Richter & de Lange 2019 (η² 0.19–0.34); Larsson & Smith 2012 (Q1). — [PMC6731093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731093/); [PMC3278317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278317)
- Additive: Marzecová 2018 N1pc (BF10 = 33,230 for main-effects model); Garrido 2018; Grotheer & Kovács 2015 (per Feuerriegel 2021); Alilović 2019 P1/N1 (interaction F = 0.317; N1 p = 0.06). — [PMC5758810](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5758810/); [Garrido DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhx087); [Feuerriegel preprint](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/y7639); [PMC6484894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6484894)
- Opposite-direction late interaction: Alilović 2019 P3a (prediction effect larger unattended; F(2,18) = 8.831). — [PMC6484894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6484894)
- Dissociable mechanisms: Wyart 2012 (Q4). — [PMC3295248](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3295248/)
- Theory behind "gain on PE": Feldman & Friston 2010, "Attention, uncertainty, and free-energy", *Front Hum Neurosci* 4:215 (verified citation). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2010.00215)
- Gordon, Tsuchiya, Koenig-Robert & Hohwy: EEG hierarchical frequency tagging; preprint "Expectation and attention increase the integration of top-down and bottom-up signals in perception through different pathways" (bioRxiv 2018, doi 10.1101/446948, verified); the published 2019 version is cited by Press et al. 2020 but its journal was NOT verified here (UNVERIFIED journal). Abstract: "both expectation and attention enhance integration between top-down and bottom-up signals" via "distinct yet related mechanisms". — [bioRxiv](https://doi.org/10.1101/446948)

### Inferences
- The interaction form depends on latency and level: early sensory components are additive or null; late components (≥ 300 ms) show interactions; fMRI (temporally blind) shows interactions whose sign varies with stimulus class and paradigm. For a serial word-reader, the analogue is that first-pass word recognition would be modulated additively by relevance and probability, and the probability × relevance interaction would appear only at the integration/decision stage.

### Gaps
- No study manipulated attention, expectation AND a neutral baseline with time-resolved recording and N ≥ 30 in vision; the "reversal" finding therefore remains a single-study result.
