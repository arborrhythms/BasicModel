## 1. The perceptual span: how far does useful information extend from the fixated word?

### Takeaway
The span of effective vision in alphabetic reading is small and asymmetric: ~3–4 characters left of fixation to ~14–15 right; letter/word-length information is used out to 14–15, but word identification (the "word identification span") is limited to roughly 7–8 characters right of fixation, i.e., the fixated word plus (usually) the next one. The span is set by attention and processing load, not acuity: it shrinks with foveal difficulty, is reversed in Hebrew, is 1 left/2–3 right in Chinese, and nothing is taken from the line below.

### Cited Findings
- Primary (classic). The gaze-contingent moving-window paradigm was introduced by McConkie & Rayner (1975), *Perception & Psychophysics*, 17(6), 578–586 — [Crossref record](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03203972). The boundary paradigm was introduced by Rayner (1975), *Cognitive Psychology*, 7(1), 65–81 — [Crossref record](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(75)90005-5).
- Review. "skilled readers of English and other alphabetic writing systems obtain useful information from an asymmetric region extending roughly 3–4 character spaces to the left of fixation ... to about 14–15 character spaces to the right of fixation" — [Rayner 2009, QJEP 62(8):1457–1506, p. 1462](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review. "if readers have the fixated word and the word to the right of fixation available on a fixation (and all other letters are replaced with visually similar letters), they are not aware that the words outside of the window are not normal, and their reading speed only decreases by about 10%. If two words to the right of fixation are available within the window, there is very little slowdown in reading (Rayner et al., 1982)" — [Rayner 2009, p. 1464](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461). The primary source is Rayner, Well, Pollatsek & Bertera (1982), *Perception & Psychophysics*, 31(6), 537–550 — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03204186).
- Review. Parafoveal magnification (enlarging letters with eccentricity to cancel acuity loss) left the rightward span at "about 14 letter spaces"; "attention and ongoing processing constraints, and not visual acuity, determine how much information can be obtained on each eye fixation in reading" — [Rayner 2009, pp. 1464–1465](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review. "Readers do not utilize information from lines below the currently fixated line" (in reading; they do in visual search) — [Rayner 2009, p. 1465](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review. Chinese: "perceptual span extends 1 character to the left of fixation to 2–3 characters to the right"; Hebrew: span "asymmetric and larger to the left of fixation"; beginning and dyslexic readers have smaller spans; older readers' span "slightly smaller and less asymmetric" — [Rayner 2009, p. 1465](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Primary. Henderson & Ferreira (1990), *JEP: LMC*, 16(3), 417–429: "less parafoveal information was acquired when processing in the fovea was difficult. We conclude that the perceptual span is variable and attentionally constrained" (abstract) — [PubMed/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037//0278-7393.16.3.417).
- Review (the 1998 Psych Bull). Rayner (1998), *Psychological Bulletin*, 124(3), 372–422, covers "(b) the perceptual span, (c) integration of information across saccades, (d) eye movement control" (abstract) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.124.3.372). The "word identification span ~7–8 characters right" figure is the standard reading of this review; a 2024-era NSF-indexed study restates it as perceptual span "between 10 and 14 characters" and word identification span "to eight characters to the right of fixation" for hearing and deaf readers — [NSF PAR record 10617623](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10617623-role-perceptual-word-identification-spans-reading-efficiency-evidence-from-hearing-deaf-readers). I could not read Rayner 1998 itself; treat the exact "7–8" wording as attributed to Rayner 1998 via secondary sources (see Gaps).
- Review. Moving-mask paradigm (foveal letters masked, only parafovea available): "reading is very difficult, if not impossible" — [Rayner 2009, p. 1465](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review. Removing inter-word spaces slows reading "by as much as 30–50%"; saccade targeting uses word-length/space information ("where" decisions are low-level; "when" decisions are lexical) — [Rayner 2009, p. 1469](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).

### Inferences
- The attended window at any instant is essentially word n plus word n+1 (two-word window ≈ normal reading speed). Letter-level information extends further right (to ~14–15 characters) but serves saccade targeting (word boundaries, length), not identification.
- The span is a function of attention/processing load (Henderson & Ferreira; Rayner's parafoveal-magnification argument), consistent with an architecture in which attention gates what is processed rather than acuity alone.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full text of Rayner (1998) to quote the exact "word identification span 7–8 characters" sentence; the figure is restated by secondary sources and Rayner 2009's "fixated word + one word to the right ≈ 10% slowdown" is the operational equivalent.
- McConkie & Rayner 1975's own numeric estimates were not read first-hand (Crossref record only).

## 2. Parafoveal preview benefit: which levels of representation, in which languages, under what conditions?

### Takeaway
A valid preview of word n+1 saves ~30–50 ms on the subsequent fixation. Orthographic (letter identity/position), abstract letter, and phonological codes are integrated across the saccade; morphological preview benefit exists in Hebrew but not English; semantic preview benefit is robust in German and Chinese, historically absent in English, and in English appears only for synonyms, capitalised targets, plausible previews, high-constraint contexts, or highly skilled readers. Preview is gated by foveal load (harder fixated word → less preview) and, in German, by preview time (≥~125 ms of pre-target fixation).

### Cited Findings
- Review (the central review requested). Schotter, Angele & Rayner (2012), "Parafoveal processing in reading", *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*, 74(1), 5–35: "discusses the levels of representation at which words are processed (orthographic, phonological, morphological, lexical, syntactic, and semantic) ... word skipping, parafoveal-on-foveal effects, and n+1 and n+2 preview benefit effects" (abstract/record) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-011-0219-2); [Bournemouth record](https://staffprofiles.bournemouth.ac.uk/display/journal-article/168409). Full text could not be retrieved (Springer blocked automated access); the level-by-level facts below are therefore taken from Rayner 2009 and the primary studies.
- Review. "The size of this preview benefit is typically of the order of 30–50 ms" — [Rayner 2009, p. 1466](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review. "readers do not combine a literal representation of the visual information across saccades ... Rather, information about the beginning and ending letters of words ... and orthographic codes ... is integrated across saccades, as are abstract letter codes and phonological information" — [Rayner 2009, pp. 1466–1467](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review (English semantic preview negative, as of 2009). "it is quite interesting that semantic information is not [integrated across saccades] (Altarriba, Kambe, Pollatsek, & Rayner, 2001; Hyönä & Häikiö, 2005; Rayner, Balota, & Pollatsek, 1986; Rayner et al., 1980a) ... words that typically produce priming in a standard naming or lexical decision task (e.g., the prime word tune primes the target word song) do not yield priming when the prime word is in parafoveal vision" — [Rayner 2009, p. 1467](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review (morphology). "there is also no evidence that morphological information is integrated across saccades when reading English ... readers of Hebrew do integrate morphological information across saccades" — [Rayner 2009, p. 1467](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review (foveal load). "If the fixated word is difficult to process, readers get little or no preview benefit from the word to the right of fixation (Henderson & Ferreira, 1990; Kennison & Clifton, 1995; White, Rayner, & Liversedge, 2005a)" — [Rayner 2009, p. 1467](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Primary (German, fast-priming). Hohenstein, Laubrock & Kliegl (2010), *JEP: LMC*, 36(5), 1150–1170: semantic preview benefit when a semantically related parafoveal word was available during the initial 125 ms of the pre-target fixation; with a salient target location, significant parafoveal semantic priming at 80 ms (as summarised in search-result text; title/venue verified via Crossref) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020233).
- Primary (German, boundary paradigm). Hohenstein & Kliegl (2014), *JEP: LMC*, 40(1), 166–190, three experiments: "Fixation durations on the target were shorter for semantically related than unrelated previews ... A semantic preview benefit was obtained under both [capitalised and all-lowercase] conditions ... fixation durations on the target were reliably different for all 4 types of preview [identical, related, unrelated, nonword]. Semantic preview benefits were greater for pretarget fixations closer to the boundary (large preview space) and ... for long pretarget fixation durations (long preview time)" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033670).
- Primary (English synonyms). Schotter (2013), *JML*, 69(4), 619–633: "semantic preview benefit can be observed in English when the preview and target are synonyms", but not for semantic associates; argued to "depend on several factors—attentional resources, depth of orthography, and degree of similarity between preview and target" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2013.09.002).
- Primary (English capitalisation). Rayner & Schotter (2014), *JEP: HPP*, 40(4), 1617–1628: gaze-duration preview benefit for capitalised targets: identical preview 30.42 ms shorter than unrelated, semantically related 10.73 ms shorter (marginal); for lowercase targets: identical 13.42 ms, semantic n.s. "Semantic preview benefit was not observed when the preview/target word was not capitalized, but was observed when the preview/target word was capitalized." — [PMC4147079](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4147079).
- Primary (English, skill). Veldre & Andrews (2016), *JEP: LMC*, 42(6), 837–854; 99 adult readers: "On average, readers showed a benefit from previews high in semantic feature overlap with the target. However, reading and spelling ability yielded opposite effects ... High reading ability was associated with a semantic preview benefit that was equivalent to an identical preview on first-pass reading. High spelling ability was associated with a reduced semantic preview benefit despite an overall higher rate of skipping." (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000212).
- Primary (English, plausibility vs relatedness). Veldre & Andrews (2016), *JEP: HPP*, 42(7), 939–952: "readers benefited from a preview of a plausible word, regardless of the semantic relatedness of the preview and the target. However, there was limited evidence of a semantic relatedness benefit when the plausibility of the preview was controlled. The plausibility preview benefit was strongest for low proficiency readers" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000200).
- Primary. Veldre & Andrews (2018), "Beyond cloze probability: Parafoveal processing of semantic and syntactic information during reading", *JML*, 100, 1–17 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2017.12.002) (title/venue verified; content not read). Veldre & Andrews (2018), "How does foveal processing difficulty affect parafoveal processing during reading?", *JML*, 103, 74–90 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2018.08.001) (title/venue verified; content not read).
- Primary (contextual constraint). Schotter, Lee, Reiderman & Rayner (2015), *JML*, 83, 118–139: "we found an early (i.e., first-pass) apparent preview benefit for semantically associated previews in constrained contexts that went away in late measures ... sentence constraint ... does not operate by making a single word form expected, but rather generates expectations about what kinds of words are likely to appear ... compatible with the assumption of the E-Z Reader model that early oculomotor decisions reflect 'hedged bets'" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2015.04.005).
- Primary (plausibility, English). Schotter & Jia (2016), *JEP: LMC*, 42(12), 1839–1866: "Early reading measures were shorter for all plausible preview conditions compared to the implausible preview condition ... Readers were more likely to report the preview when they had skipped the word and not regressed to it, and when the preview was plausible ... the preview word is processed to a high level of representation [but] its influence on reading is relatively short-lived, being replaced by the target word, when fixated" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000281).
- Primary (meaning from preview). Schotter & Fennell (2019), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 26(5), 1697–1704: regressions from sentence end increased when a (skipped/previewed) word was later rendered implausible, "especially when the preview was high frequency ... linguistic information can be obtained during preview, to the point where word meaning is accessed" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-019-01662-1).
- Primary (EEG co-registration, German word lists). Dimigen, Kliegl & Sommer (2012), *NeuroImage*, 62(1), 381–393: identical previews shortened target fixations and produced an "occipitotemporal preview positivity between 200 and 280 ms"; "there was no facilitation from related previews"; N400 priming between consecutive related words appeared "only shortly (160 ms) after the second word was directly fixated" — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2012.04.006).
- Primary (English, FRPs + eye movements). Degno, Loberg, Zang, Zhang, Donnelly & Liversedge (2019), *JEP: General*, 148(3), 453–474: visual and orthographic parafoveal-on-foveal effects in both eye movements and FRPs; replicated the preview positivity (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000494).
- Primary (English, natural reading co-registration). Antúnez, Milligan, Hernández-Cabrera, Barber & Schotter (2022), *Psychophysiology*, 59(4), e13986: "replicated previous evidence of semantic parafoveal processing from highly controlled reading situations, extending their findings to more ecologically valid reading scenarios"; plausibility preview effects in FRPs "may be long-lasting" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13986).

### Inferences
- Preview is primarily sub-lexical/form-level (letters, phonology); semantic extraction from n+1 is real but conditional — on orthography (German capitals, Chinese density), plausibility/constraint, preview time (≥~80–125 ms), low foveal load, and reader skill. It is not a default lexical-semantic read of the parafovea in English.
- The preview, once extracted, can stand in for the percept when the word is skipped (readers report the preview, and regress later if it turns out implausible), but when the word is fixated the foveal percept overrides it within the same fixation (Schotter & Jia 2016).

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the full text of Schotter et al. (2012) to extract their tabulated preview-benefit sizes per level; the 30–50 ms figure is from Rayner 2009.
- Chinese semantic preview primary studies (Yan et al. 2009; Yang et al. 2010/2012) are referenced within Rayner & Schotter 2014 and Schotter & Jia 2016 but were not independently verified here.

## 3. Predictability and word skipping; the limits of lexical prediction in natural text

### Takeaway
About one third of words are skipped; length is the strongest determinant (3-letter words ~67% skipped, 7–8-letter ~20%), predictability adds ~8 percentage points on average (range 0–13%), frequency less. Predictability also shortens first-fixation/gaze durations by ~10–20 ms and shifts the whole fixation-duration distribution. But highly predictable words are rare in text (≈5% of content words, ≈19–20% of function words at cloze >0.67; the modal cloze response is the actual word for only ~21% of content words), there is no measurable misprediction cost in eye movements, and part-of-speech, inflection and semantic features are far more predictable than word identity — favouring graded pre-activation over all-or-none lexical prediction.

### Cited Findings
- Primary. Rayner, Slattery, Drieghe & Liversedge (2011), *JEP: HPP*, 37(2), 514–528; 28 eye-tracking participants, 93 norming participants; cloze of predictable targets .70/.77/.75 vs unpredictable .14/.17/.13 (short/medium/long). Skipping: short (4–6 letters) 36% predictable vs 28% unpredictable; medium (7–9) 22% vs 18%; long (10–12) 18% vs 10%. Fixation-time predictability effects: first fixation 11 ms, single fixation 13 ms, gaze 18 ms. "The length by predictability interaction never approached significance." Intro: "three letter words are skipped about 67% of the time, whereas 7–8 letter words are skipped only about 20% of the time" (Rayner & McConkie 1976) and roughly "1/3 of all words ... being initially skipped during reading" — [PMC3543826](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543826). Rayner & McConkie (1976), *Vision Research*, 16(8), 829–837 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(76)90143-7).
- Review. "content words are fixated about 85% of the time, while function words are fixated about 35% of the time ... Two factors have a big impact on skipping: word length and contextual constraint. First, the most important variable in skipping is word length ... Second, words that are highly constrained by the prior context are much more likely to be skipped ... Word frequency also has an effect on word skipping, but the effect is smaller than that of predictability (Rayner et al., 1996). While predictability influences whether or not a word is skipped, it does not influence where in the word the fixation lands" — [Rayner 2009, pp. 1461, 1471](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Review (skipped words are processed). "It is a mistake to think that if a word is skipped it is not processed. Fisher and Shebilske (1985) ... deleted all words from the passage that these readers had skipped and asked a second group of readers to read the passage. This second group of readers had a difficult time understanding the text ... the fixation prior to skipping has been shown to be inflated ... as has the fixation after the skip" — [Rayner 2009, pp. 1471–1472](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).
- Primary (classics, verified records). Ehrlich & Rayner (1981), *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 20(6), 641–655 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371(81)90220-6); Luke & Christianson report that Ehrlich & Rayner "compared highly predictable words (mean cloze score = 0.93) with non-predictable words (mean cloze score = 0.15)" — [Luke & Christianson 2016, p. 24](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2016.06.002). Balota, Pollatsek & Rayner (1985), *Cognitive Psychology*, 17(3), 364–390 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(85)90013-1). Rayner & Well (1996), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 3(4), 504–509 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03214555).
- Chapter (skipping as a function of length/predictability). Brysbaert & Vitu (1998), "Word skipping", in *Eye Guidance in Reading and Scene Perception*, 125–147 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/b978-008043361-5/50007-9). Brysbaert, Drieghe & Vitu (2005), "Word skipping: Implications for theories of eye movement control in reading", in *Cognitive Processes in Eye Guidance*, 53–78 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198566816.003.0003). Secondary summary (search-result text, not read first-hand): their meta-analysis found predictability effects on skipping "ranging from 0% to 13%, with an average of 8%", and word length explained more skipping variance than frequency or predictability — [cogprints record](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/3422/index.html). Rayner et al. 2011 characterise Brysbaert & Vitu as proposing that "word skipping is based on more coarse information" — [PMC3543826](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543826).
- Review (Staub). Staub (2015), "The effect of lexical predictability on eye movements in reading: Critical review and theoretical interpretation", *Language and Linguistics Compass*, 9(8), 311–327 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1111/lnc3.12151). Abstract (as returned by search): predictable words receive shorter fixations and are more likely to be skipped; effects present "in the earliest possible eye movement measures" imply predictability influences word recognition itself; the effects "are not due to discrete predictions of specific words, but rather to broad pre-activation of potential continuations".
- Primary (distributional shape). Staub (2011), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 18(2), 371–376: for first-fixation and single-fixation durations the ex-Gaussian μ increased for unpredictable words while τ was unaffected: "a predictability manipulation shifts the distribution of fixation durations but does not affect the degree of skew" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-010-0046-9).
- Primary (large cloze corpus). Luke & Christianson (2016), *Cognitive Psychology*, 88, 22–60: cloze for all 2,689 words (1,197 unique) in 55 passages (mean 50 words) from 470 survey participants; separate eye-tracking participants read the same texts. Mean cloze: content .13 (nouns .17, verbs .16, adjectives .07, adverbs .13), function .30, grand mean .22. Highly predictable (>0.67): content 5%, function 19% (11% overall). Target was the modal response: content 21%, function 40% (~30% overall) — "for an average of about 7 out of 10 words the word encountered was not the word most likely to be predicted" — [Luke & Christianson 2016, Table 3 (p. 30) and §4 Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2016.06.002); open PDF: https://education.illinois.edu/docs/default-source/edpsy-documents/limits-on-lexical-prediction-during-reading.pdf
- Primary (same). Part-of-speech match of cloze responses: nouns .72, verbs .72, adjectives .30, adverbs .25; ~2/3 of nouns and verbs had highly predictable word class; number inflection matched 72% (nouns), tense 78% (verbs); responses semantically related to targets (LSA above randomised baseline) — [Luke & Christianson 2016, Table 4, p. 33](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2016.06.002).
- Primary (same). Eye-movement means: skipping probability .32 (content) / .62 (function); first fixation 214/201 ms; gaze 261/216 ms; total time 314/245 ms (Table 6). "Predictability was significant (and facilitative) in all analyses"; slopes statistically indistinguishable for modal vs non-modal targets in nearly all measures; "In the eye-tracking data there was no evidence that mis-predictions come at a cost ... the presence of a more expected competitor word was found to be facilitative, rather than inhibitory" — [Luke & Christianson 2016, Table 6 (p. 34), §3.2 and §4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2016.06.002). Conclusion (§5, pp. 46–47): "(1) all-or-nothing lexical prediction does not occur often; (2) graded prediction, a more continuous pre-activation process, is a better characterization ...; (3) most predictions will be partial and even sparse, but semantic and morphosyntactic features of upcoming words are more likely to be predicted than the actual words themselves".
- Primary (2024, misprediction costs). Wong, Veldre & Andrews (2024), *QJEP*, 77(10), 2040–2064: unrelated unpredictable words in high-constraint contexts cost "8 and 12 ms on first fixation and gaze duration, respectively" (Exp. 3) with "no evidence that downstream targets showed any processing consequences"; the authors call prediction effects "small and short-lived" and note prior eye-movement evidence of prediction-error costs is "contradictory and generally elusive" — [PMC11453035](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11453035/); preprint [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/842hk).

### Inferences
- Skipping is a joint function of coarse parafoveal information (length, first letters, familiarity) and context; predictability adds roughly 4–8 percentage points to skipping on top of length, and ~10–20 ms to fixation times. The predictability effect is a shift of the whole duration distribution (Staub 2011), consistent with faster recognition rather than occasional "guessing" trials.
- Because specific-word prediction is correct for only ~2–3 words in 10 in natural text, a reading architecture cannot rely on substituting a predicted word for the percept; it can rely on predicted features (class, inflection, semantic field) to speed recognition.

### Gaps
- Brysbaert & Vitu (1998) and Brysbaert, Drieghe & Vitu (2005) could not be read first-hand (cogprints and OUP blocked access); the "0–13%, mean 8%" predictability-on-skipping figure comes from search-result text citing them and should be checked against the chapter.
- Staub (2015) full text was behind Wiley's paywall; only the abstract was available.

## 4. Serial versus parallel attention: E-Z Reader vs SWIFT/OB1-Reader, parafoveal-on-foveal and n+2 effects, and the 2019–2026 state of the debate

### Takeaway
E-Z Reader allocates attention serially (one word at a time): an early "familiarity check" (L1) on word n triggers saccade programming; completion of lexical access (L2) shifts attention to n+1; skipping occurs when L1 on n+1 finishes before the saccade program's labile stage ends. SWIFT distributes processing over a Gaussian gradient (fitted σ_right = 3.74 letters, σ_left = 2.41 letters; typically three words active) with an autonomous saccade timer modulated by foveal inhibition; OB1-Reader uses an adjustable attentional window with parallel open-bigram coding and a spatiotopic sentence representation. Eye-movement evidence alone has not settled it: lexical parafoveal-on-foveal effects are reliable in corpora but weak in experiments; n+2 preview benefit is mostly absent (except with very short n+1). The 2019–2026 picture: letters/orthography of ~3 words are processed in parallel, but there is a serial bottleneck at lexical access/recognition (dual-task and fMRI data); the transposed-word effect survives serial presentation; flanker effects stop at n±1; FRPs show syntactic effects of n+1 within 100 ms.

### Cited Findings
- Primary (E-Z Reader origin). Reichle, Pollatsek, Fisher & Rayner (1998), *Psychological Review*, 105(1), 125–157: "The major goal of the modeling is to relate cognitive processing (specifically aspects of lexical access) to eye movements in reading" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.105.1.125). Reichle, Rayner & Pollatsek (2003), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 26(4), 445–476 (E-Z Reader 7; "E-Z Reader provides the most comprehensive account of eye movement control during reading") — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X03000104). E-Z Reader 10 (post-lexical integration): Reichle, Warren & McConnell (2009), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 16(1), 1–21 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.3758/pbr.16.1.1).
- Review (mechanism, verbatim from an open-access review). "the early stage of processing, L1, corresponds to a familiarity check" ... "completion of L1 causes the oculomotor system to start programming a saccade to the next word" ... "The later stage of processing, L2, corresponds to lexical access. The completion of L2 causes attention to shift from the currently fixated word ... to the next parafoveal word" ... ### Inferences
- The two camps have converged on a layered answer: spatial attention/visual-orthographic processing spans ~3 words (n−1, n, n+1; SWIFT's σ ≈ 2.4 left / 3.7 right letters; flanker effects only at n±1), whereas lexical access/recognition is capacity-limited and behaves serially under tight timing (White et al.). Effects of n+1 on fixation n exist but are small and mainly orthographic in controlled experiments; lexical PoF effects are corpus-level and may partly reflect mislocated fixations and memory retrieval.
- For an architecture: a FOCUS region on word n plus a NEXT region on n+1 that passes sub-lexical/featural information (and a predicted feature set) matches the data better than either strict one-word serialism or a five-word parallel lexical field. Chinese and MCU evidence suggest the "unit" can be a multiword chunk.

## 5. Prediction in comprehension: graded vs all-or-none, surprisal, N400, and the cost of prediction

### Takeaway
Current theory defines prediction minimally as context changing the processor's state before input arrives, operating as graded, probabilistic pre-activation at semantic, syntactic and (weakly) form levels. The N400 scales inversely and roughly linearly with cloze; the DeLong et al. 2005 article (a/an) effect — the flagship evidence for pre-activating phonological form — did not replicate in a 9-lab N = 334 study (noun effect replicated, β = 2.22 µV/unit cloze). Reading time is a logarithmic function of probability (linear in surprisal) over six orders of magnitude (Smith & Levy 2013; Shain et al. 2024, 2.2M data points), with Brothers & Kuperberg (2021) the main linear-effect dissent — itself now contradicted by an eye-tracking replication (Buggy, Cho, Shain & Staub, 2026). Prediction is held to be facilitative but not necessary (Huettig & Mani), optional and resource-dependent (Pickering & Gambi), and eye movements show little or no misprediction cost.

### Cited Findings
- Review. Kuperberg & Jaeger (2016), *Language, Cognition and Neuroscience*, 31(1), 32–59: minimal definition — "the context influences the state of the language processing system before the bottom-up input is observed"; "More recent accounts view prediction as a graded and probabilistic phenomenon"; "the magnitude of the N400 effect evoked by an incoming word is inversely correlated with that word's probability in relation to its preceding context"; pre-activation at "syntactic structure ... phonological information ... and orthographic information"; disconfirmed strong predictions evoke "a larger anteriorly distributed late positivity"; "a mathematical equivalence between Bayesian surprise and the information theoretic construct of surprisal"; "prediction is not simply an 'add-on' ... it plays a pivotal role in driving higher level inference" — [PMC4850025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4850025/).
- Review. Huettig & Mani (2016), "Is prediction necessary to understand language? Probably not", *LCN*, 31(1), 19–31: five arguments against the necessity claim, including "not all language users appear to predict language" and "suboptimal input makes prediction often very challenging"; prediction "is strongly context-dependent and impeded by resource limitations"; "languages can be learned and understood in the absence of prediction" (abstract, via MPI/Göttingen records) — [MPI record](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item2171154/prediction-necessary-understand-language-probably-not); [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1080/23273798.2015.1072223).
- Review. Pickering & Gambi (2018), *Psychological Bulletin*, 144(10), 1002–1044: prediction "occurs at all linguistic levels from semantics to form"; prediction-by-production "takes time and resources ... many groups of comprehenders (non-native speakers, illiterates, children, and older adults) using it less"; "prediction-by-production is an optional mechanism, which is augmented by mechanisms based on association" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000158).
- Primary. DeLong, Urbach & Kutas (2005), *Nature Neuroscience*, 8(8), 1117–1121: graded N400 modulation of nouns and preceding articles by cloze — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1504). - Primary (surprisal theory). Levy (2008), "Expectation-based syntactic comprehension", *Cognition*, 106(3), 1126–1177 — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2007.05.006).

- Primary (large-scale). Shain, Meister, Pimentel, Cotterell & Levy (2024), *PNAS*, 121(10), e2307876121: six datasets (Brown SPR 35 participants/136,907 responses; Dundee ET 10/408,439 fixations; GECO ET 14/374,179; Natural Stories SPR 178/1,013,377; Natural Stories Maze 95/97,527; Provo ET 84/213,224), surprisal from 5-gram, PCFG, GPT-2, GPT-J, GPT-3 and (Provo) human cloze; "surp1 is the best performing constrained model overall, significantly outperforming both sublogarithmic models ... and superlogarithmic models"; re-analysis of Brothers & Kuperberg's data with GPT-2 "instead favor a logarithmic over a linear effect"; interpretation: "a major driver of processing cost is probabilistic inference over a (possibly vast) space of interpretations of the unfolding sentence" — [PMC10927576](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10927576).

- Primary (no misprediction cost in eye movements). Luke & Christianson (2016): "Our data provide no evidence for prediction costs in reading" and argue the N400 does not index failed-prediction cost (Van Petten & Luka 2012) while a post-N400 frontal positivity might — [Luke & Christianson 2016, §4.5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2016.06.002). Wong, Veldre & Andrews (2024): 8–12 ms costs only under strong constraint, nothing downstream — [PMC11453035](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11453035/).

### Inferences
- The field's operational notion of "expectation" is a probability distribution over features/words whose mismatch cost is logarithmic (surprisal), not a single predicted token that is confirmed or refuted. An architecture subtracting a predicted image from the percept should therefore expect graded residuals and should not expect large penalties when a specific word fails.
- Form-level (phonological/orthographic) pre-activation of a specific word is the weakest and least replicable level; semantic/syntactic feature pre-activation is the strongest.

### Gaps
- Smith & Levy's per-bit slope (ms per bit of surprisal) was not extracted; Shain et al. likewise report model comparisons rather than ms/bit.
- Huettig & Mani and Pickering & Gambi were read only at abstract level (publisher 403s).

## 6. Are unattended, masked, or unfixated words semantically processed?

### Takeaway
Outside reading, the modern consensus is Broadbent-with-updates: masked semantic (N400) priming and unconscious repetition/response priming require that attention be directed to the prime's time window and location; truly unattended stimuli produce no identification priming. Syntax, however, can be processed in an attended stream without conscious detection of the violation. Inside reading, words in the parafovea (≈2° from fixation, i.e., word n+1) do elicit graded N400 expectancy effects before being fixated, but only under conditions where attention/constraint allows it, and higher-order integration (late positivity) waits for foveation.

### Cited Findings
- Primary (review + experiments). Lachter, Forster & Ruthruff (2004), *Psychological Review*, 111(4), 880–913: "When steps were taken to ensure that irrelevant stimuli were not attended, these stimuli produced no priming effects. Hence, the authors found no evidence that unattended stimuli can be identified." (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.111.4.880).
- Primary. Kiefer & Brendel (2006), *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience*, 18(2), 184–198: masked primes "not consciously identified"; "Unrelated prime-target pairs elicited a larger N400 than related pairs ... this masked N400 priming effect was strongest when the cue interval and the stimulus onset asynchronies were short ... temporal attention to the prime is a prerequisite for obtaining masked N400 priming effects" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1162/089892906775783688) (Crossref also lists [10.1162/jocn.2006.18.2.184](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn.2006.18.2.184)).
- Primary. Naccache, Blandin & Dehaene (2002), *Psychological Science*, 13(5), 416–424: "Both response-congruity priming and physical repetition priming vanish when temporal attention is focused away from this time window" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00474).
- Primary. Batterink & Neville (2013), *Journal of Neuroscience*, 33(19), 8528–8533: cross-modal attentional-blink distraction task; "syntactic violations that were not consciously detected nonetheless produced a characteristic early neural response pattern, and also significantly delayed reaction times to a concurrent task. This early neural response was distinct from later neural activity that was observed only to syntactic violations that were consciously detected" (abstract) — [PMC3720232](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3720232). Note: the words were attended (task-relevant stream); what was absent was awareness, not attention.
- Primary (parafoveal N400, RSVP-with-flankers). Barber, van der Meij & Kutas (2013), *Psychophysiology*, 50(1), 48–59: parafoveal words "are processed in parallel with foveal words, but ... the extraction of semantic information parafoveally is a function of contextual constraint and presentation rate, most likely under high contextual constraint and at slower rates" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8986.2012.01489.x). An earlier "Parafoveal N400 effect during sentence reading" (Barber et al., 2010) is listed by the University of Lübeck repository — [record](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/parafoveal-n400-effect-during-sentence-reading/) — UNVERIFIED details (venue/pages not confirmed here).
- Primary. Stites, Payne & Federmeier (2017), *Cognitive, Affective, & Behavioral Neuroscience*, 17(3), 475–490: flankers "2° bilaterally"; "an N400 effect to the target word when it appeared in the parafovea, which was graded with respect to the target's expectancy and congruency ... when targets appeared at central fixation, this graded congruency effect was mitigated" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13415-016-0492-6).
- Primary. Payne, Stites & Federmeier (2019), *Psychophysiology*, 56(10), e13432: flankers anchored "at 2° of visual angle from the center"; "the N400 to the parafoveal presentation of the target word was graded in amplitude based on the expectancy of the parafoveal target"; the foveal N400 "was reduced in magnitude ... and showed a reduced impact of expectancy"; a late positivity to incongruity appeared "only in the plausibility judgment task ... only once words had moved into foveal vision"; conclusion: "semantic access can be initiated in parafoveal vision, whereas central foveal vision may be necessary to enact higher-order (and task-dependent) integrative processing" — [PMC6879358](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879358).
- Primary (natural reading, FRPs). Kretzschmar, Bornkessel-Schlesewsky & Schlesewsky (2009), *NeuroReport*, 20(18), 1613–1618: in "the opposite of black is white/yellow/nice", FRPs "locked to the last fixation before the critical word showed an N400 only for the nonrelated unpredicted condition (nice) ... attributed to a parafoveal mismatch between the critical word and preactivated lexical features ... the first demonstration of a parafoveally induced N400 effect" (abstract) — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1097/WNR.0b013e328332c4f4).
- Primary (natural reading, word lists). Dimigen, Kliegl & Sommer (2012): "We found no evidence that word meaning contributes to these [preview] effects" in fluent list reading; N400 priming appeared only after direct fixation — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2012.04.006).
- Review (task modulates lexical processing of fixated words). "when viewers are presented passages of text and are asked to search for a target word in the text, the frequency effect disappears (Rayner & Fischer, 1996; Rayner & Raney, 1996)" — [Rayner 2009, p. 1472](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461).

### Inferences
- "Unfixated" is not "unattended": the parafoveal word n+1 lies inside the attended window, and that is where expectancy-graded N400s and semantic preview effects arise. Evidence for semantic processing of words outside the attended window (e.g., n+2, other lines, spatially unattended flankers) is essentially absent; where attention is withdrawn in time or space, even masked-priming "automatic" effects vanish.
- Thus prediction can be compared against perception at n+1 before fixation, but the comparison is attention-gated; the architecture's masked field is consistent with the data if the NEXT region carries attention to n+1.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly tests N400/semantic effects for a word two positions ahead (n+2) in sentence reading with ERP; Angele & Rayner 2011 and Rayner 2009 (eye movements) indicate no lexical n+2 preprocessing in English.
- Barber et al. 2010 (Neuroscience Letters?) details unverified.

## 7. Synthesis for the three design questions

### Takeaway
(1) Prediction operates at the sentence/discourse level regardless of gaze, but its measurable contact with perception is confined to the attended window — word n and (if attention reaches it) word n+1; nothing lexical or semantic is extracted from n+2 or beyond, and semantic preview at n+1 is conditional. (2) Prediction does not substitute for perception: skipped words are processed from parafoveal information (coarse orthography/familiarity), prediction biases the skip decision by ~4–8 percentage points, and when preview and context conflict the parafoveal visual evidence usually wins — yet readers sometimes commit to a skip on a "hedged bet" and encode the preview (even a wrong one) rather than the target. (3) Attention in reading is best described as a narrow graded window (~3 words wide, asymmetric to the right) over which visual/orthographic processing is parallel while lexical identification is capacity-limited and effectively one word at a time.

### Cited Findings
- (1) Prediction beyond the window: Kliegl et al. 2006 — predictability of n+1 lengthens fixation n (221 vs 212 ms when n is long), interpreted as memory retrieval of n+1 cued by context, i.e., prediction touching n+1 before attention/eyes arrive — [open PDF](https://publishup.uni-potsdam.de/files/5521/phr_263_2011_12_13.pdf). Stites et al. 2017 / Payne et al. 2019 — graded expectancy N400 for n+1 at 2° — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13415-016-0492-6), [PMC6879358](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879358). Angele & Rayner 2011 — no lexical preprocessing of n+2 — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023096). Rayner 2009 — no information from the line below; two-word window ≈ normal reading — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461). Luke & Christianson 2016 — what is predicted is mostly features (POS .72, inflection 72–78%) not identity (5% of content words) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2016.06.002).
- (2) Prediction vs perception in skipping: Drieghe, Rayner & Pollatsek 2005 — "skipping rates were higher for a preview of a predictable word than for a visually similar nonword, indicating there is full recognition in parafoveal vision ... [but] significant skipping of nonwords similar to the target word, indicating skipping based on partial information" — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.31.5.954). Angele & Rayner 2013 — infelicitous parafoveal "the" skipped frequently; readers "based their skipping decision solely on the orthographic properties of the article" — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029294). Angele, Laishley, Rayner & Liversedge 2014 — "visual information about the upcoming word trumps information from the sentence context when it comes to making a skipping decision" — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0036396). Abbott, Angele, Ahn & Rayner 2015 — yet "Readers skipped predictable words more often than unpredictable words, even when the ... was presented as a parafoveal preview ... a decision to skip it can be made even if the information available parafoveally conflicts both visually and syntactically with those predictions" — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000142). Schotter & Jia 2016 — readers report the preview, not the target, when they skipped — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000281). Rayner et al. 2011 — skipping = successful L1 familiarity check, "even when a parafoveal word's identity can only be assumed based on partial parafoveal orthographic information" — [PMC3543826](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3543826). Rayner 2009 — skipped words are processed (Fisher & Shebilske 1985) and the fixations before/after a skip are inflated — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461). Rayner et al. 2011 skipping rates (36/28, 22/18, 18/10%) and Brysbaert et al. 2005 average 8% predictability effect (secondary) — see §3.
- (3) Serial vs parallel: SWIFT σ_R = 3.74 / σ_L = 2.41 letters, ~3 words active — [open PDF](https://publishup.uni-potsdam.de/files/5512/phr_254_2011_12_13.pdf); Mlinarič et al. 2025 flanker effects only at n±1 (~13 ms), "approximately three words in parallel" — [PMC12205018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205018/); White et al. 2019 — parallel up to orthography, serial lexical access, VWFA-2 single channel — [open PDF](https://courses.washington.edu/viscog/publications/White_TrendsCognSci_2019.pdf); Snell et al. 2023 — syntactic effect of n+1 at 100 ms post-fixation — [Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2023.02.004); Huang & Staub 2023 and Milledge et al. 2023 — transposed-word effect survives serial presentation — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-022-02150-9), [PMC10600278](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10600278/); Rayner 2009 and Zang 2019 — lexical PoF effects corpus-bound and weak — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902816461), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/vision3040050).

### Inferences
- A FOCUS region on word n and a NEXT region on n+1 reproduces the empirical attended window (two-word window ≈ 90–100% reading speed). The NEXT region should pass coarse form (length, initial letters, familiarity) and receive the predicted feature distribution; semantic contact at n+1 should be conditional (constraint, load, skill), not default.
- Expectation should be feature-level and graded (surprisal-like), subtracted at n and at n+1 inside the window; outside the window the prediction can exist but has nothing to be subtracted from (no percept), and the literature gives no evidence of perception there.
- Skipping in the architecture should be modelled as an early familiarity decision on the NEXT region's coarse evidence biased by prediction (E-Z Reader L1 / SWIFT "need not be fully identified"), with the preview content standing in for the percept of the skipped word and remaining correctable by later regressions.

### Gaps
- No study quantifies how often a skipped word's encoded identity is wrong in natural reading (Schotter & Jia 2016 gives probe-report rates for manipulated previews only).
- The precise time course by which a prediction formed during fixation n biases processing of n+1 (Kliegl et al.'s memory-retrieval account vs E-Z Reader's attention-shift account) remains contested; no decisive 2020–2026 experiment was found.
